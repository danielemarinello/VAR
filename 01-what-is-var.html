3  VAR intro

3.1 What is VAR?

3 starting key info:

  1. VAR (Vector AutoRegressive) is a multivariate1 linear time series model, designed to capture the joint dynamics2 of multiple time series through an interconnected system.

  2. VARs, by default, treat each endogenous variable3 in the system as a function of lagged values of all endogenous variables. Exogenous variables enter only when explicitly added.

  3. VARs offer a simple4 and flexible alternative to traditional multiple equation models5.

3.1.1 A closer look: joint dynamics, interconnected system and endogeneity

In a VAR the variables are not studied side by side. They form a single system, where each one drives the others and is driven by them. This happens because each variable appears on the left-hand side of its own equation and on the right-hand side of the others. Since every variable has its own equation, each one is explained within the system, and none can be treated as exogenous: this is exactly what endogeneity means here.
The link is not a one-off cross-effect, nor does it run in one direction only: it is mutual, and it propagates forward indefinitely.

Example:

To fix ideas, let inflation (\(\pi\)) and GDP each depend on both their own lag and the other’s lag. Since the true data-generating process is unknown, the relationship is left as a generic function:

\[\begin{aligned} \pi_t &= f(\pi_{t-1},\, gdp_{t-1}) \\ gdp_t &= f(gdp_{t-1},\, \pi_{t-1}) \end{aligned}\]

Following a single cross-channel makes the mechanism visible and more intuitive. Take the \(gdp \to \pi\) link:

\[gdp_{t-1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_t \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+3} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots\]

This single thread is real, but on its own it understates the structure. Own lags run alongside these cross-links, and each own lag is itself shaped by the other variable one period earlier: \(gdp_{t-1}\) already carries \(\pi_{t-2}\). Every value is therefore fed, at every period, by both variables at once; as shown in the animation below, where horizontal arrows are own lags (persistence) and diagonal arrows are cross-lags (spillovers).

This self-sustaining feedback loop is what makes the system genuinely joint, and it is the aspect most often underestimated when first learning VAR.

3.2 VAR from a mathematical POV

A VAR is an \(n\)-equation, \(n\)-variable linear model in which each variable is explained by its own lagged values, plus current (initially this term will missed, it appears later in the text) and past values of the remaining \(n-1\) variables.

Formally, let \(Z_t\) be a vector collecting the values of \(n\) variables at time \(t\), for instance inflation, output gap, and the interest rate. A vector autoregressive process of order \(p\), written VAR(\(p\)), generalizes the one-variable AR(\(p\)) process to \(n\) variables:

\[Z_t = c + \Phi_1 Z_{t-1} + \Phi_2 Z_{t-2} + \cdots + \Phi_p Z_{t-p} + w_t\] {#eq-varp-general}

where:

\[Z_t = \begin{bmatrix} z_{1t} \\ z_{2t} \\ \vdots \\ z_{nt} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Phi_j = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11}^{(j)} & \cdots & \phi_{1n}^{(j)} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \phi_{n1}^{(j)} & \cdots & \phi_{nn}^{(j)} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w_t = \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} \\ w_{2t} \\ \vdots \\ w_{nt} \end{bmatrix}\] {#eq-varp-components}

The model describes the evolution of \(Z_t\) as a function of its own past realizations \(Z_{t-1}, \dots, Z_{t-p}\) and of a vector of white noise errors \(w_t\).

The parameter counts shown in the three box follow a simple rule. Each of the \(n\) equations carries a constant plus \(n\) coefficients per lag, plus the autoregressive part that grows as \(pn^2\): linearly in the lags, but quadratically in the variables, giving \(n + pn^2\) across the system.
The covariance matrix \(\Omega\) adds a further \(n(n+1)/2\) parameters. So a modest VAR with \(n = 4\) and \(p = 4\) already needs \(5 + 4\cdot 16 = 69\) coefficients, estimated from macroeconomic samples that are rarely long.
This is the curse of dimensionality of VARs, and the reason parsimony in \(n\) and \(p\) is central to specification.

3.2.1 The error structure

The white-noise assumption on \(w_t\) is central and deserves to be stated precisely. Each error has zero mean and finite variance, is serially uncorrelated within equation over time but it may be correlated across equations at the same time. This distinction is crucial and recurs multiple time throughout the text.
Formally:

\[\operatorname{E}[w_t] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{E}[w_t w_\tau'] = \begin{cases} \Omega & \text{if } t = \tau \\ 0 & \text{if } t \neq \tau \end{cases}\] {#eq-varp-errors}

More precisely:

  1. Along the time dimension, the errors are uncorrelated with their own past and with all the right-hand-side variables, the lagged \(Z\)’s. This absence of serial correlation is what makes OLS, applied equation by equation, consistent.
  2. Across equations, by contrast, \(\Omega\) is not assumed to be diagonal6, so the errors of different equations can be correlated at the same date \(t\). This non-zero off-diagonal is the trace left by the contemporaneous links between the variables: how in reality they influence each other in \(t\). This is the reason why, starting to be precise with name and classification, ?eq-varp-general is called reduced-form VAR: the contemporaneous variables, the ones quoted earlier, are apparently absent from the right-hand side.

This cross-equation correlation is not a nuisance to be removed but the core point of the reduced form. The general picture can now begin to take shape. Contemporaneous variables are kept off the right-hand side because their presence would make the model impossible to estimate, as shown below; what is estimated is therefore the so-called reduced form.
Yet the trace of how the variables influence one another within period \(t\) does not vanish: it hides in the off-diagonal entries of the variance-covariance matrix, which record the covariances between the errors of the different equations at the same date. That single object, \(\Omega\), is where the whole contemporaneous structure now lives, and it deserves examination on its own.

3.2.1.1 The error variance covariance matrix

The matrix \(\Omega\) introduced in ?eq-varp-errors is the contemporaneous variance-covariance matrix of the error vector \(w_t\). In a reduced-form VAR it is the only place where the contemporaneous relations among the variables survive. Its entries are what needs to be examined: the diagonal and the off-diagonal carry different information, and seeing why, from the algebra, clarifies what the matrix actually measures.

Its content follows directly from the definition \(\Omega = \operatorname{E}[w_t w_t']\). To see why it holds variances on the diagonal and covariances off it, take \(n = 2\) and expand. The outer product of the error vector with itself is

\[w_t w_t' = \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} \\ w_{2t} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} & w_{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t}^2 & w_{1t} w_{2t} \\ w_{2t} w_{1t} & w_{2t}^2 \end{bmatrix},\]

so taking expectations entry by entry gives

\[\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{E}[w_{1t}^2] & \operatorname{E}[w_{1t} w_{2t}] \\ \operatorname{E}[w_{2t} w_{1t}] & \operatorname{E}[w_{2t}^2] \end{bmatrix}.\]

The white-noise assumption fixes what each entry is. Because the errors have zero mean, the general definitions of variance and covariance simplify:

\[\operatorname{Var}(w_{it}) = \operatorname{E}[w_{it}^2] - \underbrace{(\operatorname{E}[w_{it}])^2}_{=\,0} = \operatorname{E}[w_{it}^2], \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(w_{it}, w_{jt}) = \operatorname{E}[w_{it} w_{jt}] - \underbrace{\operatorname{E}[w_{it}]\operatorname{E}[w_{jt}]}_{=\,0} = \operatorname{E}[w_{it} w_{jt}].\]

The diagonal entries \(\operatorname{E}[w_{it}^2]\) are therefore the variances \(\sigma_i^2\), and the off-diagonal entries \(\operatorname{E}[w_{it} w_{jt}]\) the contemporaneous covariances \(\sigma_{ij}\).

Written out, for clarification, for \(n = 2\) and \(n = 3\) the matrix takes the following form:

\[\Omega_{(n=2)} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Omega_{(n=3)} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \sigma_{12} & \sigma_{13} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2 & \sigma_{23} \\ \sigma_{13} & \sigma_{23} & \sigma_3^2 \end{bmatrix}\] {#eq-omega-examples}

Two things can be read from this structure directly. As said before the diagonal collects the variances \(\sigma_i^2\), one per equation, measuring how volatile each equation’s own shock is; instead the off-diagonal collects the contemporaneous covariances \(\sigma_{ij}\), measuring whether and in which direction the shocks of equations \(i\) and \(j\) move together at the same date.
The matrix is symmetric, since \(\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}\), so the co-movement between two equations is a single number, not two. A diagonal \(\Omega\), with every \(\sigma_{ij} = 0\), would describe a system whose shocks are mutually uncorrelated, each equation disturbed on its own; in macroeconomic data this is the exception rather than the rule.

A non-zero off-diagonal is thus the footprint of the contemporaneous links among the variables. As long as non-zero values are present, \(w_t\) cannot be called a vector of structural shocks: the terms used instead are innovations, reduced-form residuals, or one-step-ahead forecast errors of the system. Structural shocks require the off-diagonal entries to be zero (called orthogonality7), since only then would the errors be mutually uncorrelated and thus separately interpretable.

Separate interpretability is what the whole exercise rests on. Tracing the effect of a shock means asking how the system reacts when one disturbance moves and everything else stays still, the usual ceteris paribus clause. Correlated errors make that question unanswerable: if \(w_{1t}\) and \(w_{2t}\) move together, a movement in the first never occurs alone, and what is observed is the joint response to both, not the isolated effect of one. Only orthogonal shocks can be moved one at a time, and only then does the question have a well-defined answer. Reaching them starting from reduced-form residuals is the goal of identification: Chapter 5 examines what structural shocks are used for and the ways of recovering them.

The number of equations is \(n\), and it is \(n\) alone that fixes the size of the error vector and therefore of its covariance matrix. This count matters for identification, since it is exactly the number of pieces of information the data supply about the contemporaneous structure, and it is what any set of identifying restrictions will have to work against.

3.3 Why VARs are simple, flexible, and (so) widely used

A standard ARDL model[^01-what-is-var-8] is inherently unidirectional: the researcher must decide, a priori, which variable belongs on the left-hand side and which on the right. Influence runs one way because the equation was written that way.

That choice is not free. Placing \(x\) on the right without giving it an equation of its own amounts to assuming that \(x\) is exogenous: determined outside the model, unaffected by \(y\), and in particular uncorrelated with the error of the equation. Unidirectionality is legitimate only if that assumption holds. The true data-generating process (DGP), however, is always unobservable, so there is no way to verify whether the imposed direction reflects reality or is merely a convenient simplification.

Whether the assumption is defensible depends on the field. In microeconometrics a natural temporal ordering often settles the chicken-and-egg problem: education precedes wages by years, so wages today cannot alter schooling completed a decade ago, and treating education as exogenous is defensible. In macroeconometrics such precedence rarely exists. Aggregate variables move together within the observation window, and agents are forward-looking, so expectations about the future feed back into the present. To continue the earlier example, GDP and inflation are mutually determined simultaneously, within the same period, and neither can be declared prior to the other.

Traditional macroeconometric models resolved the difficulty by decree. To identify those systems, some variables were declared exogenous and entire blocks of regressors were excluded from particular equations, with coefficients set to zero a priori. Sims (1980) objected that such restrictions did not follow from genuine economic knowledge but were simplifications adopted because they made the model tractable, and, being untestable against an unobservable DGP, could not be defended. He called them “incredible restrictions”.

VARs answer by dropping the constraint of unidirectionality altogether. Every variable receives its own equation, nothing is excluded a priori, and the data are left to reveal the dynamics. This is what makes the VAR both simpler and more flexible: it is essentially atheoretical in structure, treating every variable as potentially endogenous rather than imposing in advance who (exogenous) influences whom (endogenous).

The gain, however, is more subtle than it appears. The VAR does not solve simultaneity; it sidesteps it, at the beginning, with the reduced-form. Estimation is carried out with a reduced-form in which only lagged variables appear on the right-hand side, and the contemporaneous relation, rather than vanishing, hiddes into the correlation between the residuals of different equations. What the VAR achieves is therefore not the removal of the problem but its exposure: where traditional models buried the contemporaneous structure in untested assumptions, the VAR leaves it visible and measurable. Restrictions become necessary again only at the stage of identification, where they are stated openly. The next section follows this passage step by step.

3.3.1 A closer look: simultaneity and the reduced form

The claim that GDP and inflation are determined “within the same period” deserves a closer look, because it is the reason a VAR is estimated in the particular form it is. This section gives the intuition and the essential steps; the full treatment, including how the underlying shocks are recovered, is the subject of Chapter 5.

Simultaneity means that two variables are set together, in the same instant, each responding to the other within the period. This differs from the lagged feedback of the animation above, where GDP at \(t-1\) moved inflation at \(t\): there the influence travelled through time, from one period to the next, and posed no difficulty for estimation. Simultaneity is sharper. Output lifts prices and inflation lifts output (the erosion of purchasing power drives consumption today to avoid paying higher prices tomorrow) in the same quarter , so neither can be placed first on the right-hand side. This is the chicken-and-egg problem in its strict form in macroeconometric.

The rest of this section unpacks the problem in four steps: writing the honest model, seeing why it cannot be estimated, finding the way out, and tracking where the simultaneity ends up.

  1. The true and honest model:

If \(x\) and \(y\) are truly determined together, the faithful description places each contemporaneous variable on the other’s right-hand side:

\[\begin{aligned} x_t &= \beta_{12}\, y_t + \gamma_{11}\, x_{t-1} + \gamma_{12}\, y_{t-1} + \eta_{1t}\\ y_t &= \beta_{21}\, x_t + \gamma_{21}\, x_{t-1} + \gamma_{22}\, y_{t-1} + \eta_{2t} \end{aligned} \tag{3.1}\]

The shocks \(\eta_{1t}, \eta_{2t}\) are the structural shocks, also called primitive impulses, structural innovations, or fundamental shocks in the literature. They are the genuine economic driving forces, a monetary-policy shock or a technology shock for instance, and they are uncorrelated with each other.

Moving the contemporaneous terms \(\beta_{12}\, y_t\) and \(\beta_{21}\, x_t\) to the left-hand side gathers everything dated \(t\) on one side. This regrouping is what the matrix \(B\) encodes, and it is done for computational convenience: with the period-\(t\) variables collected together, the system can later be solved in one step by premultiplying by \(B^{-1}\). In matrix form it becomes \(B\, Z_t = \Gamma\, Z_{t-1} + \eta_t\), with

\[\begin{aligned} &B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\beta_{12} \\ -\beta_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Gamma = \begin{bmatrix} \gamma_{11} & \gamma_{12} \\ \gamma_{21} & \gamma_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \eta_t = \begin{bmatrix} \eta_{1t} \\ \eta_{2t} \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &Z_t = \begin{bmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Z_{t-1} = \begin{bmatrix} x_{t-1} \\ y_{t-1} \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \tag{3.2}\]

Each piece plays a distinct role. \(Z_t\) stacks the two contemporaneous variables, and \(Z_{t-1}\) their first lags. On the left, \(B\) holds the contemporaneous links: ones on the diagonal, so each variable enters its own equation with coefficient one, and the off-diagonal entries \(-\beta_{12}, -\beta_{21}\) carrying the simultaneous effects moved over from the right. On the right, \(\Gamma\) holds the lag coefficients, mapping how yesterday’s values \(Z_{t-1}\) feed today’s, and \(\eta_t\) collects the two structural shocks. If the off-diagonal entries of \(B\) were zero, \(B\) would reduce to the identity and there would be no simultaneity left to deal with.

  1. Why it cannot be estimated:

OLS delivers a reliable and consistent estimate under one essential condition: each regressor must be uncorrelated with the error of its own equation8. Consider estimating the first equation of Equation 3.1 and apply that single test. The contemporaneous regressor is \(y_t\) and the error is \(\eta_{1t}\). Trace the chain: \(y_t\) is set by the second equation, where it depends on \(x_t\); and \(x_t\) is set by the first equation, where it carries \(\eta_{1t}\). Putting these together, \(\eta_{1t}\) moves \(x_t\), which moves \(y_t\), so \(y_t\) and \(\eta_{1t}\) move together: \(\text{Cov}(y_t, \eta_{1t}) \neq 0\). The key condition is violated; OLS then credits the regressor with movement that in reality belongs to the error, and returns a biased and inconsistent estimate. This is the classic simultaneity bias, and it is exactly why the traditional simultaneous-equation models had to rely on instrumental variables and exclusion restrictions to break the link between regressors and errors: the credibility of those restrictions is what Sims contested. The moral is clear: written explicitly into the equations, simultaneity makes the model impossible to estimate directly.

  1. The way out:

Premultiplying Equation 3.2 by \(B^{-1}\) isolates \(Z_t\) on the left. Indeed \(B^{-1} B = I\) and on the left-hand side what is left is \(Z_t\), while every term on the right is equal to the previous one multiplied by \(B^{-1}\):

\[B^{-1} B\, Z_t = B^{-1}\Gamma\, Z_{t-1} + B^{-1}\eta_t \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; Z_t = \underbrace{B^{-1}\Gamma}_{\Phi}\, Z_{t-1} + \underbrace{B^{-1}\eta_t}_{w_t} \tag{3.3}\]

The contemporaneous terms are gone: only lagged variables remain on the right-hand side and this is what makes OLS valid here. The regressors are all dated \(t-1\), while the error \(w_t\) is dated \(t\). A value already determined in the previous period cannot move together with a shock that arrives only in the current one, so regressors and error are necessarily uncorrelated and the condition of step 2 is satisfied. Each equation can therefore be estimated separately by OLS. This is the reduced-form VAR of ?eq-var1-matrix-const, and this is why estimation is simple.

  1. Where the simultaneity goes:

It does not disappear. Two properties of the reduced-form residuals must be kept apart, and they point in opposite directions.

Within each equation the residuals must be white noise: serially uncorrelated with its own past, as a well-specified model requires. Residual autocorrelation within equation signals misspecification, typically too few lags, and would call for a better specification.

Across equations, by contrast, the two residuals are correlated at the same date \(t\), because each one blends the same underlying structural shocks (as shown mathematically below). This contemporaneous cross-equation correlation is the footprint left by simultaneity: no longer explicitly visible on the equations, but hided in the correlation between residuals across equation, which leads to a variance covariance matrix in which the term outside the diagonal are not zero. Residual correlation across equation call for identification.

This is precisely why the reduced form alone cannot answer structural questions. The goal of impulse-response analysis is to trace the effect of a single structural shock in isolation, \(\eta_{1t}\) on its own. But the observed residuals never move one at a time: since \(w_{1t}\) and \(w_{2t}\) are correlated, a movement in the first equation arrives together with a movement in the second, and what one observes is the combined effect of both, not the bare effect of a single shock. Recovering the separate structural shocks from these blended residuals is the task of identification, taken up in Chapter 5.

Mathematical and numerical example:

  • The mechanism in the two-variable VAR(1):

The blending is visible by writing \(w_t = B^{-1}\eta_t\) in extended form, step by step.
The inverse of \(B\) is

\[B^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \beta_{12} \\ \beta_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix},\]

so the residual vector, defined as \(w_t = B^{-1}\eta_t\), expands as

\[\begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} \\ w_{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \beta_{12} \\ \beta_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \eta_{1t} \\ \eta_{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} \eta_{1t} + \beta_{12}\,\eta_{2t} \\ \beta_{21}\,\eta_{1t} + \eta_{2t} \end{bmatrix}.\]

Row by row, each reduced-form residual is a linear combination of both structural shocks:

\[\begin{aligned} w_{1t} &= \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}}\left(\eta_{1t} + \beta_{12}\,\eta_{2t}\right)\\[4pt] w_{2t} &= \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}}\left(\beta_{21}\,\eta_{1t} + \eta_{2t}\right) \end{aligned} \tag{3.4}\]

  • A numerical case:

Let \(\beta_{12} = \beta_{21} = 0.5\), so that \(1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21} = 0.75\) and

\[w_{1t} = \tfrac{4}{3}\,\eta_{1t} + \tfrac{2}{3}\,\eta_{2t}, \qquad w_{2t} = \tfrac{2}{3}\,\eta_{1t} + \tfrac{4}{3}\,\eta_{2t}.\]

Suppose the structural shocks are uncorrelated and have unit variance, the most innocent case possible. Then

\[\operatorname{Cov}(w_{1t}, w_{2t}) = \tfrac{4}{3}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{4}{3} = \tfrac{16}{9}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(w_{1t}) = \operatorname{Var}(w_{2t}) = \tfrac{16}{9} + \tfrac{4}{9} = \tfrac{20}{9},\]

giving a correlation of \(\tfrac{16}{9} \big/ \tfrac{20}{9} = \tfrac{16}{20} = 0.80\). Two perfectly independent structural shocks produce observed residuals correlated at eighty percent: the correlation reflects no link between the primitive causes, only the simultaneity encoded in \(B\).

It does not disappear. Writing \(w_t = B^{-1}\eta_t\) out for the two-variable VAR(1) shows why. With

\[B^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \beta_{12} \\ \beta_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix},\]

each reduced-form residual is a linear combination of both structural shocks:

\[\begin{aligned} w_{1t} &= \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}}\left(\eta_{1t} + \beta_{12}\,\eta_{2t}\right)\\[4pt] w_{2t} &= \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21}}\left(\beta_{21}\,\eta_{1t} + \eta_{2t}\right) \end{aligned} \tag{3.5}\]

Two properties must be kept apart. Within each equation the residual is white noise: serially uncorrelated with its own past, as a well-specified model requires. Residual autocorrelation would instead signal misspecification, typically too few lags, and would call for a different specification rather than for identification. Across equations, however, the two residuals are correlated at the same date \(t\), because they share the same underlying shocks \(\eta_{1t}\) and \(\eta_{2t}\). This contemporaneous cross-equation correlation is the footprint left by simultaneity, now stored in the residual covariance matrix rather than on the right-hand side.

A numerical case makes the size of the effect concrete. Let \(\beta_{12} = \beta_{21} = 0.5\), so that \(1 - \beta_{12}\beta_{21} = 0.75\) and

\[w_{1t} = \tfrac{4}{3}\,\eta_{1t} + \tfrac{2}{3}\,\eta_{2t}, \qquad w_{2t} = \tfrac{2}{3}\,\eta_{1t} + \tfrac{4}{3}\,\eta_{2t}.\]

Suppose the structural shocks are uncorrelated and have unit variance, the most innocent case possible. Then

\[\operatorname{Cov}(w_{1t}, w_{2t}) = \tfrac{4}{3}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{4}{3} = \tfrac{16}{9}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(w_{1t}) = \operatorname{Var}(w_{2t}) = \tfrac{16}{9} + \tfrac{4}{9} = \tfrac{20}{9},\]

giving a correlation of \(\tfrac{16}{9} \big/ \tfrac{20}{9} = \tfrac{16}{20} = 0.80\).
So two perfectly independent structural shocks produce observed residuals correlated at eighty percent, magnitude apart, it is pretty intuitive since both are linear combination of the same variable.

This is precisely why the reduced form alone cannot answer structural questions. The goal of impulse-response analysis is to trace the effect of a single structural shock in isolation, \(\eta_{1t}\) on its own. But the observed residuals never move one at a time: because \(w_{1t}\) and \(w_{2t}\) are correlated, a movement attributed to the first equation arrives together with a movement in the second, and the two effects cannot be separated by inspection. Hitting the system with \(\eta_{1t}\) alone is not something the reduced form lets one observe directly. Recovering the separate structural shocks from these blended residuals is the task of identification, taken up in Chapter 5.

In short, simultaneity is never removed, only moved: the structural shocks enter uncorrelated, the matrix \(B^{-1}\) blends them, and what remains observable are reduced-form residuals correlated across equations.     .style("display", "flex").style("gap", "6px").style("align-items", "center")
    .style("margin-bottom", "6px");
  const mkBtn = (label) => controls.append("button").text(label)
    .style("padding", "3px 10px").style("font-size", "13px")
    .style("border", "1px solid #ccc").style("border-radius", "6px")
    .style("background", "#fff").style("cursor", "pointer");
  const playBtn = mkBtn("Pause");
  const resetBtn = mkBtn("Reset");
  const prevBtn = mkBtn("\u2039");
  const nextBtn = mkBtn("\u203A");
  const holder = container.append("div");

  let f = 0, playing = true;

  function flow(svg, x0, y0, x1, y1, mode) {
    const col = mode === "hot" ? amber : mode === "fired" ? "#c9c9c9" : faint;
    const w = mode === "hot" ? 3.5 : 1.6;
    svg.append("line").attr("x1", x0).attr("y1", y0).attr("x2", x1).attr("y2", y1)
      .attr("stroke", col).attr("stroke-width", w)
      .attr("opacity", mode === "faint" ? 0.7 : 1)
      .attr("marker-end", "url(#sm-" + mode + ")");
  }

  function halfPath(cx, cy, side) {
    const sweep = side === "L" ? 0 : 1;
    return "M " + cx + " " + (cy - R) + " A " + R + " " + R + " 0 0 " + sweep + " " + cx + " " + (cy + R) + " Z";
  }

  function update() {
    holder.selectAll("*").remove();
    const svg = holder.append("svg")
      .attr("viewBox", [0, 0, width, height])
      .attr("width", width)
      .attr("style", "max-width:100%;height:auto;");

    const defs = svg.append("defs");
    [["faint", faint], ["fired", "#c9c9c9"], ["hot", amber]].forEach(([id, c]) => {
      defs.append("marker").attr("id", "sm-" + id)
        .attr("viewBox", "0 0 10 10").attr("refX", 9).attr("refY", 5)
        .attr("markerWidth", 7).attr("markerHeight", 7).attr("orient", "auto")
        .append("path").attr("d", "M0 0 L10 5 L0 10 z").attr("fill", c);
    });

    const showBox = f >= 1, aFired = f >= 2, bFired = f >= 3, showCorr = f >= 4;

    // column headers
    svg.append("text").attr("x", sourceX).attr("y", headerY).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
      .attr("font-size", 12).attr("fill", "#666").text("structural shocks");
    svg.append("text").attr("x", resX).attr("y", headerY).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
      .attr("font-size", 12).attr("fill", "#666").text("reduced-form residuals");

    // mixing box
    if (showBox) {
      svg.append("rect").attr("x", boxL).attr("y", boxCY - boxH / 2)
        .attr("width", boxW).attr("height", boxH).attr("rx", 10)
        .attr("fill", "#fafafa").attr("stroke", "#bbb").attr("stroke-width", 1.5);
      svg.append("text").attr("x", boxCX).attr("y", boxCY - 4).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
        .attr("font-size", 20).attr("font-weight", "bold").attr("fill", "#444").text("B\u207B\u00B9");
      svg.append("text").attr("x", boxCX).attr("y", boxCY + 14).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
        .attr("font-size", 9).attr("fill", "#888").text("inverse of");
      svg.append("text").attr("x", boxCX).attr("y", boxCY + 26).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
        .attr("font-size", 9).attr("fill", "#888").text("simultaneity matrix");
    }

    // flows
    if (showBox) {
      const inA = f === 2 ? "hot" : (f > 2 ? "fired" : "faint");
      const inB = f === 3 ? "hot" : (f > 3 ? "fired" : "faint");
      const out = (f === 2 || f === 3) ? "hot" : (f >= 4 ? "fired" : "faint");
      flow(svg, sourceX + R, row1Y, boxL, inTopY, inA);
      flow(svg, sourceX + R, row2Y, boxL, inBotY, inB);
      flow(svg, boxR, outTopY, resX - R, row1Y, out);
      flow(svg, boxR, outBotY, resX - R, row2Y, out);
    }

    // sources (pure structural shocks)
    [[row1Y, colA, "\u03B7\u2081"], [row2Y, colB, "\u03B7\u2082"]].forEach(([yy, cc, lab]) => {
      svg.append("circle").attr("cx", sourceX).attr("cy", yy).attr("r", R)
        .attr("fill", cc).attr("stroke", cc).attr("stroke-width", 2);
      svg.append("text").attr("x", sourceX).attr("y", yy - R - 8).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
        .attr("font-size", 15).attr("font-weight", "bold").attr("fill", cc).text(lab);
    });

    // "uncorrelated" marker between sources (green, blinking, OK)
    svg.append("line").attr("x1", sourceX).attr("y1", row1Y + R + 4)
      .attr("x2", sourceX).attr("y2", row2Y - R - 4)
      .attr("stroke", "#cbcbcb").attr("stroke-width", 1.5).attr("stroke-dasharray", "4 4");
    const uncorrLabel = svg.append("text").attr("x", sourceX - R - 10).attr("y", boxCY + 4)
      .attr("text-anchor", "end").attr("font-size", 11).attr("font-weight", "bold")
      .attr("fill", okGreen).text("uncorrelated \u2713");
    uncorrLabel.append("animate").attr("attributeName", "opacity")
      .attr("values", "1;0.25;1").attr("dur", "1.4s").attr("repeatCount", "indefinite");

    // residuals (blends)
    [[row1Y, "w\u2081"], [row2Y, "w\u2082"]].forEach(([yy, lab]) => {
      svg.append("circle").attr("cx", resX).attr("cy", yy).attr("r", R)
        .attr("fill", coldFill).attr("stroke", "none");
      if (aFired) svg.append("path").attr("d", halfPath(resX, yy, "L")).attr("fill", colA).attr("opacity", 0.9);
      if (bFired) svg.append("path").attr("d", halfPath(resX, yy, "R")).attr("fill", colB).attr("opacity", 0.9);
      svg.append("circle").attr("cx", resX).attr("cy", yy).attr("r", R)
        .attr("fill", "none").attr("stroke", (aFired || bFired) ? "#666" : coldStroke).attr("stroke-width", 2);
      svg.append("text").attr("x", resX).attr("y", yy - R - 8).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
        .attr("font-size", 15).attr("font-weight", "bold")
        .attr("fill", (aFired || bFired) ? "#444" : "#aaa").text(lab);
    });

    // "correlated" marker between residuals (red, blinking, NOT ok)
    if (showCorr) {
      svg.append("line").attr("x1", resX).attr("y1", row1Y + R + 4)
        .attr("x2", resX).attr("y2", row2Y - R - 4)
        .attr("stroke", noRed).attr("stroke-width", 2).attr("stroke-dasharray", "5 3");
      const corrLabel = svg.append("text").attr("x", resX + R + 10).attr("y", boxCY + 4)
        .attr("text-anchor", "start").attr("font-size", 11).attr("font-weight", "bold")
        .attr("fill", noRed).text("correlated \u2717");
      corrLabel.append("animate").attr("attributeName", "opacity")
        .attr("values", "1;0.25;1").attr("dur", "1.4s").attr("repeatCount", "indefinite");
    }

    // caption + counter
    svg.append("text").attr("x", width / 2).attr("y", captionY).attr("text-anchor", "middle")
      .attr("font-size", 13).attr("fill", "#333").text(frames[f].caption);
    svg.append("text").attr("x", width - 16).attr("y", captionY).attr("text-anchor", "end")
      .attr("font-size", 11).attr("fill", "#bbb").text((f + 1) + "/" + total);
  }

  playBtn.on("click", () => { playing = !playing; playBtn.text(playing ? "Pause" : "Play"); });
  resetBtn.on("click", () => { f = 0; update(); });
  prevBtn.on("click", () => { playing = false; playBtn.text("Play"); f = (f - 1 + total) % total; update(); });
  nextBtn.on("click", () => { playing = false; playBtn.text("Play"); f = (f + 1) % total; update(); });

  const timer = setInterval(() => { if (playing) { f = (f + 1) % total; update(); } }, 1600);
  invalidation.then(() => clearInterval(timer));

  update();
  return container.node();
}

3.4 How VAR is used in macroeconometrics?

VAR offers a coherent and credible framework for four distinct tasks: data description, forecasting, structural inference and policy analysis.

For forecasting and data description, a reduced-form VAR is sufficient: forecasting only requires the conditional mean of future values given the past, with no need to take a stance on the underlying structural relationships among the variables.

For structural inference and policy analysis, however, the reduced-form VAR is not enough, these tasks require an identified VAR9. In this context, the goal is to analyze the impact of structural shocks. This is done with impulse-response analysis10 and with forecast error variance decomposition11.

Two main ways to identify a VAR, and so to identify shocks’ effects:

Recursive ordering: impose a causal chain among variables (via Cholesky decomposition), assuming variable 1 affects all others within the period, variable 2 affects all but variable 1, and so on.
Structural approach: impose restrictions derived from economic theory instead of an arbitrary mechanic ordering.

More about both approaches later in Chapter 5.

  1. Multivariate: VAR models several variables jointly, not just one. Each variable depends on its own lags and on the lags of all other variables in the system. (Univariate AR: only own past values. VAR: own past + others’ past.)↩︎

  2. Joint dynamics: All variables move together and influence each other over time, not “multiple” series side by side.↩︎

  3. Endogenous vs. exogenous: an endogenous variable is determined within the system, the model explains it, and it can be both cause and effect of the others. An exogenous variable is taken as given from outside the system, it may influence the endogenous variables but is never explained by them.↩︎

  4. Estimation is simple in the sense that each equation can be estimated separately by OLS.↩︎

  5. Sims (1980) objected that traditional simultaneous equation models require knowing a priori which variables are exogenous and which regressors belong on the right-hand side of each equation. He regarded such identifying restrictions as incredible, and proposed treating all variables symmetrically as endogenous instead.↩︎

  6. A square matrix is diagonal when every entry off the main diagonal is zero, so only the entries \(\omega_{ii}\) running from top-left to bottom-right can be non-zero. For the covariance matrix \(\Omega = \operatorname{E}[w_t w_t']\), the diagonal entry \(\omega_{ii} = \operatorname{Var}(w_{it})\) is the variance of equation \(i\)’s error, while the off-diagonal entry \(\omega_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(w_{it}, w_{jt})\) is the covariance between the errors of equations \(i\) and \(j\) at the same date. A diagonal \(\Omega\) would therefore mean every such covariance is zero, that is, the errors of different equations are mutually uncorrelated. Here \(\Omega\) is not diagonal: the off-diagonal entries are generally non-zero, which is precisely the contemporaneous cross-equation correlation left behind by the simultaneity among the variables.↩︎

  7. Two random variables are orthogonal here when their contemporaneous covariance is zero, \(\operatorname{Cov}(w_{it}, w_{jt}) = 0\), since with zero-mean errors this coincides with \(\operatorname{E}[w_{it} w_{jt}] = 0\). Orthogonal residuals would give a diagonal \(\Omega\), each error moving independently of the others. The reduced-form residuals fail this: their off-diagonal covariances are non-zero, so they are not orthogonal. Structural shocks, by contrast, are orthogonal by construction, which is exactly what identification must recover.↩︎

  8. OLS attributes to a regressor all the co-movement between that regressor and the dependent variable. When the regressor also moves with the error, part of the error’s effect is mistakenly assigned to the regressor: the estimated coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) absorbs the influence of the error instead of measuring the regressor’s own effect. The estimate then does not converge to the true value even in large samples, which is what inconsistent means.↩︎

  9. Identified VAR: identification means separating correlation from causation. Correlation alone cannot tell us which shock caused what and identification requires assumptions that should be based on economic theory. Remember: The reduced-form residuals are correlated across equations (attention: not inside equations).↩︎

  10. Impulse-response function (IRF): Traces the effect of a one-time shock to one variable on the current and future values of all variables in the system, holding everything else constant. It answers: “if variable X is hit by a shock today, how does variable Y react today, next period, two periods ahead, …?”↩︎

  11. Forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD): At each forecast horizon, splits the forecast error variance of a variable into the share explained by shocks to itself versus shocks to each of the other variables. It answers: “how much of the uncertainty in forecasting X comes from X’s own shocks, and how much from shocks to the other variables?”↩︎
