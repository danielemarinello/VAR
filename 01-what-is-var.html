3.0.0.1 What is VAR?

VAR (Vector AutoRegressive) is a multivariate1 linear time series model, designed to capture the joint dynamics2 of multiple time series, through an interconnected system.

The interconnection is not a one-off cross-effect: it propagates forward indefinitely. To fix ideas, consider a simple example. Let inflation (\(\pi\)) and GDP each depend on both their own lag and the other’s lag. Since the true data-generating process is unknown, the relationship is left as a generic function:

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= f(\pi_{t-1},\, gdp_{t-1}) \\ gdp_t &= f(gdp_{t-1},\, \pi_{t-1}) \end{aligned} \]

Tracing a single channel makes the mechanism visible. Following only the \(gdp \to \pi\) link — own-lag effects operate in parallel, in exactly the same way:

\[ gdp_{t-1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_t \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+3} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots \]

A shock to GDP today does not stop after reaching inflation next period. It feeds back into GDP the period after, which feeds back into inflation again, and so on without end. This self-sustaining feedback loop is what makes the system genuinely joint — and it is the aspect most often underestimated when first learning VAR. The diagram below animates the transmission: an impulse originating in GDP alternates between the two variables as time advances, never settling.

VARs treat each endogenous variable in the system as a function of lagged values of all endogenous variables.

VARs offer a simple and flexible alternative to traditional multiple equation models.

3.0.0.2 Why is VAR simple and flexible, so useful and used?

A standard AR and ARDL model3 is inherently unidirectional: the researcher must decide, a priori, which variable belongs on the left-hand side and which on the right. But the true data-generating process (DGP) is unobservable — we can never verify whether the relationship we impose reflects reality, or is simply a convenient assumption.

VARs sidestep this problem by allowing for simultaneity: every variable can affect, and be affected by, every other variable, with no need to decide in advance who is endogenous and who is exogenous. Since every equation shares the same regressors — lags of all variables — each can be estimated independently via OLS.

In microeconomics, this chicken-and-egg problem can sometimes be resolved through natural temporal ordering: education precedes wages, so treating it as exogenous is defensible. In macroeconomics, this precedence rarely exists — GDP, inflation, and interest rates are determined simultaneously, within the same period.

This is what makes the VAR both simpler and more flexible: it is essentially atheoretical in structure, treating every variable as potentially endogenous and letting the data reveal the dynamics, rather than imposing a priori who (exogenous) influences whom (endogenous).

3.0.0.3 VAR from a mathematical POV

A VAR is an \(n\)-equation, \(n\)-variable linear model in which each variable is in turn explained by its own lagged values, plus current and past values of the remaining \(n-1\) variables.

Consider a bivariate VAR(1) with \(x_t\) and \(y_t\). In equation-by-equation form:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= c_1 + \phi_{11}\, x_{t-1} + \phi_{12}\, y_{t-1} + w_{1t} \\ y_t &= c_2 + \phi_{21}\, x_{t-1} + \phi_{22}\, y_{t-1} + w_{2t} \end{aligned} \tag{3.1}\]

Defining:

\[ Z_t = \begin{bmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} & \phi_{12} \\ \phi_{21} & \phi_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w_t = \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} \\ w_{2t} \end{bmatrix} \tag{3.2}\]

the VAR(1) can be written compactly in matrix form as:

\[ Z_t = \Phi\, Z_{t-1} + w_t \tag{3.3}\]

or, including the constant vector \(c = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 & c_2 \end{bmatrix}'\):

\[ Z_t = c + \Phi\, Z_{t-1} + w_t \tag{3.4}\]

We can verify that Equation 3.3 is equivalent to Equation 3.1 by expanding the matrix product:

\[ \Phi Z_{t-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} & \phi_{12} \\ \phi_{21} & \phi_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_{t-1} \\ y_{t-1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} x_{t-1} + \phi_{12} y_{t-1} \\ \phi_{21} x_{t-1} + \phi_{22} y_{t-1} \end{bmatrix} \tag{3.5}\]

3.0.0.4 How VAR is used in macroeconometrics?

VARs offer a coherent and credible framework for four distinct tasks: data description, forecasting, structural inference and policy analysis.

For forecasting and data description, a reduced-form VAR is sufficient: forecasting only requires the conditional mean of future values given the past, with no need to take a stance on the underlying structural relationships among the variables.

For structural inference and policy analysis, however, the reduced-form VAR is not enough — these tasks require an identified VAR4. In this context, the goal is to analyze the impact of structural shocks. This is done with impulse-response analysis5 and with forecast error variance decomposition6.

  1. Multivariate: VAR models several variables jointly, not just one. Each variable depends on its own lags and on the lags of all other variables in the system. (Univariate AR: only own past values. VAR: own past + others’ past.)↩︎

  2. Joint dynamics: This is the first key concept. VAR accounts for the fact that all variables move together and influence each other over time — not “multiple” series side by side, but one interconnected system.

    The interconnection is not a one-off cross-effect, it propagates forward indefinitely. To fix ideas, consider the following basic example: Suppose inflation (\(\pi\)) and GDP depend on both their own lag and each other’s lag, without knowing the exact data generation process, we are accounting for a generic function:

    \[\begin{aligned} \pi_t &= f(\pi_{t-1},\, gdp_{t-1}) \\ gdp_t &= f(gdp_{t-1},\, \pi_{t-1}) \end{aligned}\]

    We can see that the cross-variable link propagates over time following the following scheme (own-lag effects work the same way, in parallel), follow just the \(gdp \to \pi\) channel:

    \[gdp_{t-1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_t \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+3} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots\]

    A shock to GDP today doesn’t stop after hitting inflation next period — it feeds back into GDP the period after, which feeds back into inflation again, and so on. This self-sustaining feedback loop is what makes the system truly joint, and it’s the part most often underestimated when first learning VAR.↩︎

  3. ARDL (AutoRegressive Distributed Lag): a single-equation model in which a dependent variable is explained by its own lags (the autoregressive part) plus current and past values of one or more explanatory variables (the distributed-lag part). For a single regressor:

    \[ y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i\, y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{q} \beta_j\, x_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]

    The roles are fixed a priori: \(y\) is the dependent variable and \(x\) is treated as exogenous — exactly the unidirectional assumption a VAR avoids.↩︎

  4. Identified VAR: identification means separating correlation from causation — the reduced-form residuals are correlated across equations, but correlation alone cannot tell us which shock caused what. Two main ways to identify a VAR, and so to identify shocks’ effects:

    • Recursive ordering: impose a causal chain among variables (via Cholesky decomposition), assuming variable 1 affects all others within the period, variable 2 affects all but variable 1, and so on.
    • Structural approach: impose restrictions derived from economic theory instead of an arbitrary ordering.
    ↩︎

  5. Impulse-response function (IRF): Traces the effect of a one-time shock to one variable on the current and future values of all variables in the system, holding everything else constant. It answers: “if variable X is hit by a shock today, how does variable Y react today, next period, two periods ahead, …?”↩︎

  6. Forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD): At each forecast horizon, splits the forecast error variance of a variable into the share explained by shocks to itself versus shocks to each of the other variables. It answers: “how much of the uncertainty in forecasting X comes from X’s own shocks, and how much from shocks to the other variables?”↩︎
