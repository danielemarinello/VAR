3.1 What is VAR?

3 starting key info:

  1. VAR (Vector AutoRegressive) is a multivariate1 linear time series model, designed to capture the joint dynamics2 of multiple time series through an interconnected system.

  2. VARs, by default, treat each endogenous variable3 in the system as a function of lagged values of all endogenous variables. Exogenous variables enters only when explicitly added.

  3. VARs offer a simple4 and flexible alternative to traditional multiple equation models5.

3.1.1 A closer look: joint dynamics, interconnected system and endogeneity

In a VAR the variables are not studied side by side: they form a single system in which each one both drives and is driven by the others.

The interconnection is neither a one-off cross-effect nor unidirectional, it is mutual. Each variable enters the other’s equation, so influence runs in both directions and it propagates forward indefinitely.
Each variable appears on the left-hand side of its own equation and on the right-hand side of the others: it is at once explained by the system and a driver of it. Since every variable has its own equation, none is left unexplained and none can be treated as exogenous. This is exactly what endogeneity means here

Example:

To fix ideas, let inflation (\(\pi\)) and GDP each depend on both their own lag and the other’s lag. Since the true data-generating process is unknown, the relationship is left as a generic function:

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= f(\pi_{t-1},\, gdp_{t-1}) \\ gdp_t &= f(gdp_{t-1},\, \pi_{t-1}) \end{aligned} \]

Following a single cross-channel makes the mechanism visible and more intuitive. Take the \(gdp \to \pi\) link (own-lag effects run in parallel, in exactly the same way):

\[ gdp_{t-1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_t \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \pi_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; gdp_{t+3} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots \]

This single thread, though, understates the structure. Own lags run alongside the cross-links, and an own lag is itself a product of the other variable one period earlier: \(gdp_{t-1}\) already carries \(\pi_{t-2}\). Every value is therefore fed, at every period, by both variables at once, as shown in the animation below, where horizontal arrows are own lags (persistence); diagonal arrows are cross-lags (spillovers).

This self-sustaining feedback loop is what makes the system genuinely joint, and it is the aspect most often underestimated when first learning VAR.

3.2 Why is VAR simple and flexible, so useful and used?

A standard AR or ARDL model6 is inherently unidirectional: the researcher must decide, a priori, which variable belongs on the left-hand side and which on the right. But the true data-generating process (DGP) is always unobservable, we can never verify whether the relationship we impose reflects reality, or is simply a convenient assumption.

VARs sidestep this problem by allowing for simultaneity, avoiding the constraint of unidirectionality. As shown above every variable can affect, and be affected by, every other variable, with no need to decide in advance who is endogenous and who is exogenous. Since every equation shares the same regressors (lags of all variables) each can be estimated independently via OLS.

Also because in microeconomics, this chicken-and-egg problem can sometimes be resolved through natural temporal ordering: education precedes wages, so treating it as exogenous is defensible. In macroeconomics, this precedence rarely exists, following the previous example: GDP and inflation are determined simultaneously, within the same period.

This is what makes the VAR both simpler and more flexible: it is essentially atheoretical in structure, treating every variable as potentially endogenous and letting the data reveal the dynamics, rather than imposing a priori who (exogenous) influences whom (endogenous).

3.3 VAR from a mathematical POV

A VAR is an \(n\)-equation, \(n\)-variable linear model in which each variable is in turn explained by its own lagged values, plus current and past values of the remaining \(n-1\) variables.

Example:

The easiest case is a VAR(1), where the number inside parenthesis indicates the number of lags, with just two variable \(x_t\) and \(y_t\).

It could be expressed in equation-by-equation form:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= c_1 + \phi_{11}\, x_{t-1} + \phi_{12}\, y_{t-1} + w_{1t} \\ y_t &= c_2 + \phi_{21}\, x_{t-1} + \phi_{22}\, y_{t-1} + w_{2t} \end{aligned} \tag{3.1}\]

Or in matrix form:

\[ Z_t = c + \Phi\, Z_{t-1} + w_t \tag{3.2}\]

Where:

\[ Z_t = \begin{bmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} & \phi_{12} \\ \phi_{21} & \phi_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w_t = \begin{bmatrix} w_{1t} \\ w_{2t} \end{bmatrix} \tag{3.3}\]

It is easy to verify that Equation 3.2 is equivalent to Equation 3.1 by expanding the matrix product:

\[ \Phi Z_{t-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} & \phi_{12} \\ \phi_{21} & \phi_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_{t-1} \\ y_{t-1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11} x_{t-1} + \phi_{12} y_{t-1} \\ \phi_{21} x_{t-1} + \phi_{22} y_{t-1} \end{bmatrix} \tag{3.4}\]

3.4 How VAR is used in macroeconometrics?

VAR offers a coherent and credible framework for four distinct tasks: data description, forecasting, structural inference and policy analysis.

For forecasting and data description, a reduced-form VAR is sufficient: forecasting only requires the conditional mean of future values given the past, with no need to take a stance on the underlying structural relationships among the variables.

For structural inference and policy analysis, however, the reduced-form VAR is not enough — these tasks require an identified VAR7. In this context, the goal is to analyze the impact of structural shocks. This is done with impulse-response analysis8 and with forecast error variance decomposition9.

Two main ways to identify a VAR, and so to identify shocks’ effects:

Recursive ordering: impose a causal chain among variables (via Cholesky decomposition), assuming variable 1 affects all others within the period, variable 2 affects all but variable 1, and so on. Structural approach: impose restrictions derived from economic theory instead of an arbitrary mechanic ordering.

  1. Multivariate: VAR models several variables jointly, not just one. Each variable depends on its own lags and on the lags of all other variables in the system. (Univariate AR: only own past values. VAR: own past + others’ past.)↩︎

  2. Joint dynamics: All variables move together and influence each other over time, not “multiple” series side by side.↩︎

  3. Endogenous vs. exogenous: an endogenous variable is determined within the system, the model explains it, and it can be both cause and effect of the others. An exogenous variable is taken as given from outside the system, it may influence the endogenous variables but is never explained by them.↩︎

  4. Estimation is simple in the sense that each equation can be estimated separately by OLS. The number of parameters, however, grows quickly with the number of variables and lags, leading to the curse of dimensionality typical of time series data.↩︎

  5. Sims (1980) objected that traditional simultaneous equation models require knowing a priori which variables are exogenous and which regressors belong on the right-hand side of each equation. He regarded such identifying restrictions as incredible, and proposed treating all variables symmetrically as endogenous instead.↩︎

  6. ARDL (AutoRegressive Distributed Lag): a single-equation model in which a dependent variable is explained by its own lags (the autoregressive part) plus current and past values of one or more explanatory variables (the distributed-lag part). For a single regressor:

    \[ y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i\, y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{q} \beta_j\, x_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]↩︎

  7. Identified VAR: identification means separating correlation from causation. The reduced-form residuals are correlated across equations (remember: not inside equations), but correlation alone cannot tell us which shock caused what. Identification requires assumptions that should be based on economic theory.↩︎

  8. Impulse-response function (IRF): Traces the effect of a one-time shock to one variable on the current and future values of all variables in the system, holding everything else constant. It answers: “if variable X is hit by a shock today, how does variable Y react today, next period, two periods ahead, …?”↩︎

  9. Forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD): At each forecast horizon, splits the forecast error variance of a variable into the share explained by shocks to itself versus shocks to each of the other variables. It answers: “how much of the uncertainty in forecasting X comes from X’s own shocks, and how much from shocks to the other variables?”↩︎
