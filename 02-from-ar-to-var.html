4  Tracing the effect of a shock

4.1 From chapter 3 to chapter 4, the bridge:

What happens to output and prices when a demand shock hits? How far do inflation and employment move after a monetary tightening, and for how long? What does a sudden rise in oil prices do to the rest of the system? These are the questions macroeconometrics is built to answer.
The reasons for building a VAR at all, beyond data description and forecasting, are structural inference (“What is the causal effect of a shock on a variable?”) and policy analysis (“What would happen if the policy maker were to follow a different rule?”), and both require the ability to trace how variables respond to a shock.

Chapter 3 showed why the reduced form cannot answer them as it stands. It is estimable, but its residuals are correlated: moving one moves the others as well, so no ceteris paribus interpretation is available and the effect of a single shock can never be isolated. A demand shock cannot be spoken of on its own, and the same holds for others. Recovering the structural shocks from the reduced-form ones is the task of identification, taken up in Chapter 5.

Before that, this chapter builds the machinery that makes it possible to read the effect of a shock on macroeconomic variables. The VAR model is rewritten so that every value of the system appears as a weighted sum of past shocks, the weights being exactly the trajectory a single shock traces over time. Once that object is in hand, the reason identification matters becomes concrete: the trajectory is only economically meaningful if the shock being traced is a structural one.

Example:

To get a clear initial idea of what is going to be discussed, see the mechanism at work. Consider a bivariate system in the spirit of the previous chapter, with the output gap and inflation each depending on their own lag and on the other’s lag. Everything is in equilibrium, all variables sitting at their long-run values, until a single shock arrives at horizon zero and hits one equation.

Following what happens next makes the propagation of a shock across a (simple) interconnected system visible. At impact only the variable that was hit moves. One period later the disturbance has reached the other equation through the cross-lag, and from there it travels back, since the second variable feeds the first in turn. What began as a one-off disturbance to one equation becomes a trajectory for the whole system, and the interactive below traces it horizon by horizon.

The four sliders control the coefficients of the two equations: the two own coefficients govern how long each variable holds on to its own past, the two cross coefficients how strongly each equation feeds the other.

  1. The transmission takes a period:
    In the animation the shock reaches the second variable only one horizon after impact, never at the moment it arrives. There is therefore the implicit assumption that no contemporaneous transmission occurs in either direction. An assumption that is convenient now, to understand how the mechanism works, but almost certainly false, as the previous chapter argued. Restoring the within-period links means restoring the matrix \(B\) that contains the contemporaneous coefficients, here the assumption is basically \(B = I\).

  2. The cross-coefficients are what makes the system a system:
    Setting both of them to zero splits the model into two univariate AR processes that happen to be plotted on the same axes. A shock to the output gap then stays in the output gap, inflation never moves, and each variable decays at its own rate with nothing passing between them. What the multivariate structure adds is precisely the return channel: the disturbance leaves the equation it hit, comes back one period later altered by the other variable, and the response that results is not something either equation could have produced on its own.

  3. The choice of where the shock hits is itself the problem:
    The two buttons produce different trajectories, which is unsurprising: an inflation shock is not an output shock. What is worth noticing is what the button actually does. It moves one shock by one unit and holds the other at zero (exactly as if it was a structural shock). In the data that event never occurs, because the reduced-form shocks are correlated: when one moves, the other typically moves with it. The button performs an experiment that the reduced form does not license. It ramains useful here to provide an intuition of what is going to be studied.

Try pushing the coefficients up. 4.2 How to trace the dynamic effect of a shock?

Recursive substitution, dynamic multiplier and Wold representation (infinite moving-average) are usually understood as separate topics. They are not: they are three views of a single question “how does a shock propagate through time?”.
The interactive above already showed the response to a shock, but how is it obtained? The answer comes first in the scalar AR(1) world, where every step is intuitive and easy to understand, and is then replayed in the matrix VAR world, where the algebra is heavier but the logic is identical. The reward is a clean bridge: the moving-average weights, the dynamic multipliers, and the impulse responses are the same object.

3 key definitions:

  1. Recursive substitution: unwinding the model until \(y_t\) is written in terms of past shocks.

  2. Dynamic multiplier: the effect of one shock, horizon by horizon. This is the impulse response.

  3. Wold representation: under the stationarity assumption the influence of the initial value, present in the recursive substitution, fades to zero and only the shock sum survives, this is the infinite moving-average form, whose coefficients are the multipliers of point 2.

4.3 Part A - The scalar case: AR(1)

4.3.1 Recursive substitution

Starting with a first-order autoregressive process (the constant is dropped, since it would only shift the mean), with \(w_t\) the classic white-noise shock:

\[ y_t = \phi\, y_{t-1} + w_t, \qquad w_t \sim \text{WN}(0,\sigma^2) \tag{4.1}\]

The idea is to rewrite \(y_t\) in another form, by substituting the process repeatedly into itself. The model holds at every date, so \(y_{t-1}\) can itself be written in terms of \(y_{t-2}\), and \(y_{t-2}\) in terms of \(y_{t-3}\), and so on:

\[ y_{t-1} = \phi\, y_{t-2} + w_{t-1}, \qquad y_{t-2} = \phi\, y_{t-3} + w_{t-2}, \qquad \dots \]

Each substitution moves one period further back: a further shock enters the expression and the power of \(\phi\) rises by one, step after step, until the chain reaches the initial value \(y_{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \phi\, y_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &= \phi\,\underbrace{(\phi\, y_{t-2} + w_{t-1})}_{y_{t-1}} + w_t = \phi^{2} y_{t-2} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &= \phi^{2}\underbrace{(\phi\, y_{t-3} + w_{t-2})}_{y_{t-2}} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t = \phi^{3} y_{t-3} + \phi^{2}w_{t-2} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &\;\;\vdots \\[2pt] &= \phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1} + \phi^{\,t}w_0 + \cdots + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \end{aligned} \tag{4.2}\]

4.3.2 The dynamic multiplier and impulse response function

Since Equation 4.3 writes \(y_t\) as a linear function of the shocks, the effect of a single shock can be read off directly. This is the key result obtained by rewriting the process through recursive substitution.

Restarting the count at an arbitrary date \(t\), following the same logic applied above:

\[ y_{t+j} = \phi^{\,j+1} y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{j}\phi^{\,i}\, w_{t+j-i} \tag{4.4}\]

and differentiating with respect to the shock \(w_t\):

\[ \frac{\partial\, y_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t} = \phi^{\,j} \tag{4.5}\]

This defines the dynamic multiplier: the response of \(y\), \(j\) periods ahead, to a one-unit shock today. Two features matter:

  1. It depends only on the horizon \(j\), not on the date \(t\): the system reacts to a shock in the same way no matter when it lands.
  2. This is exactly an impulse-response function, tracing \(\phi^{\,j}\) for \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) draws the system’s response to an impulse.

The shape depends entirely on \(\phi\):

  • \(0<\phi<1\): monotone geometric decay toward zero;
  • \(-1<\phi<0\): decay with alternating sign (damped oscillation);
  • \(\phi>1\): explosive monotone growth;
  • \(\phi<-1\): explosive oscillation.

The dividing line is \(|\phi|<1\): inside it a shock’s effect eventually dies out and the system is stable; outside it the effect grows without bound, the process is explosive. The borderline \(\phi=1\) is the random walk1, where \(\phi^{\,j}=1\) for every \(j\) and a shock’s effect is permanent.

4.3.3 Stationarity

The condition \(|\phi|<1\) has appeared as the dividing line between decaying and explosive responses. It deserves a name and a definition, because everything that follows rests on it.

The distinction to be drawn at the outset is between stationary and non-stationary processes, and the question behind it is simple: does the probability distribution of the series change over time? If the distribution is the same at every date, the process is stationary; if it changes, the process is not. Strict stationarity requires the entire distribution to remain unchanged, which is a demanding condition. What is used in practice is a weaker version, covariance stationarity, which restricts only the first two moments and rests on three conditions

The first is a constant unconditional mean. Whether the series is observed at date \(t\) or at \(t+j\), its mean is the same number \(\mu\):

\[ \operatorname{E}[y_t] = \operatorname{E}[y_{t+j}] = \mu \quad \text{for every } t, j \]

The second is a constant and finite variance. The dispersion around that mean does not grow or shrink with the time passing:

\[\operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \operatorname{Var}(y_{t+j}) = \gamma_0 < \infty\]

The third condition is the substantive one: the covariance depends only on the distance between observations, not on when they occur. Two observations four periods apart have the same covariance whether they fall in 2008 and 2012 or in 1975 and 1979. What matters is the separation \(j\), not the dates:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(y_t, y_{t+j}) = \gamma_j \quad \text{for every } t \tag{4.6}\]

Taken together, the three conditions say that the process has no privileged dates. Its behaviour is the same wherever one looks along the sample, which is what makes it possible to learn about the process from a single realisation: every stretch of the series carries information about the same underlying mechanism.

For the AR(1) the three conditions hold precisely when \(|\phi|<1\), and the reason is already visible in the multiplier \(\phi^{\,j}\). A shock arriving today still contributes \(\phi^{\,j}\) to the value \(j\) periods later, so the variance of \(y_t\) is the accumulated contribution of every past shock. When the weights decay the accumulation settles at a finite number; when they do not, it grows without bound and no constant variance exists. The formal derivation is set out at the end of this part, once the moving-average form is available.

Stationarity is therefore not a technical footnote but the condition under which a shock has a finite and fading effect. Without it, the impulse response would not describe a temporary deviation from a stable path but a permanent shift, and the whole exercise of tracing a shock and watching it die out would lose its meaning.

4.3.4 From recursive substitution to the Wold representation

Now let the process have been running not from date \(-1\) but from the infinite past, that is, let \(t\to\infty\) in Equation 4.3. When \(|\phi|<1\) the term \(\phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1}\) vanishes, and only the shock sum survives:

\[ y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = w_t + \phi\, w_{t-1} + \phi^{2} w_{t-2} + \cdots \tag{4.7}\]

This is the MA(\(\infty\)), or Wold representation, where the current value \(y_t\) is written as a weighted sum of all past shocks. It is nothing more than recursive substitution pushed to its limit under stationarity, and its weights \(\phi^{\,i}\) are exactly the dynamic multipliers of Equation 4.5.

The same object is reached algebraically with the lag operator \(L\) (with \(L\,w_t = w_{t-1}\)): writing Equation 4.1 as \((1-\phi L)\,y_t = w_t\) and inverting,

\[ y_t = \frac{w_t}{1-\phi L} = \Big(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\phi^{\,i} L^{i}\Big) w_t, \tag{4.8}\]

which reproduces Equation 4.7 term by term. The geometric series converges only when \(|\phi|<1\). If \(|\phi| \ge 1\) the weights do not decay and the sum of past shocks diverges: no stable MA(\(\infty\)) exists, and the process is nonstationary.

NoteDeeper dive: Deriving the variance of a stationary AR(1)

The starting point is the Wold representation of Equation 4.7, which writes the current value as a weighted sum of all past shocks:

\[y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = w_t + \phi\,w_{t-1} + \phi^{2} w_{t-2} + \cdots\]

Step 1: the variance of a weighted sum. The rule is familiar in the two-variable case, where squaring the sum produces two squared terms and one cross term:

\[\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X) + b^{2}\operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\]

The same expansion applies to any number of terms. Squaring a sum of \(n\) items produces \(n^{2}\) products: the \(n\) products of a term with itself, which give the variances, and the remaining cross products, which give the covariances:

\[\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i} a_i X_i\right) = \sum_{i} a_i^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) + \sum_{i \neq k} a_i a_k \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_k)\]

With \(a_i = \phi^{\,i}\) and \(X_i = w_{t-i}\), this gives a squared-weight term for every shock plus a cross term for every pair of distinct shocks:

\[\operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left(\phi^{\,i}\right)^{2} \operatorname{Var}(w_{t-i}) + \sum_{i \neq k} \phi^{\,i}\phi^{\,k}\,\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(w_{t-i}, w_{t-k})}_{=\,0}\]

Extending the rule from a finite sum to an infinite one requires the series to converge, which it does precisely when \(\sum_{i}\phi^{\,2i}\) is finite. This is the same condition that will make the variance finite in Step 3.

Step 2: the cross terms vanish. This is where the white-noise assumption does its work. The shocks are serially uncorrelated, so \(\operatorname{Cov}(w_{t-i}, w_{t-k}) = 0\) whenever \(i \neq k\), and every cross term disappears. Only the squared-weight terms survive, and since each shock has the same variance \(\sigma^{2}\):

\[\operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \phi^{\,2i}\,\sigma^{2} = \sigma^{2} \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \phi^{\,2i}\]

Note that squaring the weight \(\phi^{\,i}\) produces \(\phi^{\,2i}\), which is why the condition below turns out to involve \(\phi^{2}\) rather than \(\phi\).

Step 3: summing the geometric series. Writing \(\phi^{\,2i} = \left(\phi^{2}\right)^{i}\) makes the sum a geometric series of ratio \(\phi^{2}\), each term being the previous one multiplied by the same number:

\[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left(\phi^{2}\right)^{i} = 1 + \phi^{2} + \phi^{4} + \phi^{6} + \cdots\]

A geometric series \(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} r^{i}\) converges to \(1/(1-r)\) when \(|r|<1\), and diverges otherwise. The result is quick to establish. Call the sum \(S\) and write it out, then write out the same sum multiplied by \(r\):

\[ \begin{aligned} S &= 1 + r + r^{2} + r^{3} + r^{4} + \cdots\\[2pt] r\,S &= \phantom{1 + {}} r + r^{2} + r^{3} + r^{4} + \cdots \end{aligned} \]

The two lines are identical except for the leading \(1\): every power appearing in \(rS\) appears in \(S\) as well, simply shifted one place to the right. Subtracting the second line from the first therefore cancels the whole tail and leaves a single term:

\[S - r\,S = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad S\,(1-r) = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad S = \frac{1}{1-r}\]

The manipulation is legitimate only if \(S\) is a finite number to begin with, which requires the terms \(r^{i}\) to shrink towards zero, and this happens exactly when \(|r|<1\). Outside that range the argument breaks down, and visibly so: with \(r=2\) the series \(1+2+4+8+\cdots\) grows without limit, yet the formula would return \(1/(1-2)=-1\), a negative total for a sum of positive numbers.

Here \(r = \phi^{2}\), so the condition reads \(\phi^{2}<1\). This is the same as \(|\phi|<1\): squaring a number smaller than one in absolute value makes it smaller still, while squaring one larger than one makes it larger, so the two statements select the same values of \(\phi\). The absolute value is needed because squaring erases the sign, and \(\phi=-0.8\) is admissible while \(\phi=-3\) is not, even though both satisfy \(\phi<1\). The sum therefore equals \(1/(1-\phi^{2})\), and

\[\operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-\phi^{2}} \tag{4.9}\]

Step 4: reading the result. Three checks make the expression concrete. With \(\phi = 0\) the process reduces to \(y_t = w_t\) and the formula returns \(\sigma^{2}\), as it must. With \(\phi = 0.9\) it gives \(\sigma^{2}/0.19 \approx 5.3\,\sigma^{2}\): persistence amplifies the variability of the series more than fivefold, because each value still carries the contribution of many earlier shocks. As \(\phi\) approaches one the denominator approaches zero and the variance grows without bound, which is the random walk.

The same condition \(|\phi|<1\) governs the convergence of the geometric series in Equation 4.8, and this is no coincidence: both are statements about the weights \(\phi^{\,i}\) decaying fast enough to be summed. When they do not, there is no finite variance to speak of, no stable MA(\(\infty\)) representation, and no stationary process.

The three objects introduced in this part are now one. Recursive substitution unwinds the model into past shocks; the dynamic multiplier reads off the effect of one of them; the Wold representation is that unwinding taken to its limit, and its weights are those very multipliers. What made the limit possible was stationarity, which is why the same condition \(|\phi|<1\) governs the decay of the response, the convergence of the geometric series, and the finiteness of the variance.

4.4 Part A2 - From AR(1) the AR(p) - WIP - RIVEDERE TEORIA bene dall’hamilton

In the AR(1) the Wold weights turned out to be the powers \(\phi^{\,i}\), a closed form obtained almost for free from recursive substitution. That was a fortunate case. With two lags the substitution has two \(y\) terms to chase at every step, with three lags three, and the chain becomes unmanageable long before any pattern emerges.

What is needed is a method that does not depend on the number of lags. This part introduces it, and the reward is threefold: the weights of any AR(\(p\)) can be computed, the shapes the impulse response can take turn out to be far richer than in the AR(1), and the stationarity condition acquires a form that survives the move to matrices in Part B.

4.4.1 The weights are the impulse response, whatever the model

Before computing anything, it is worth establishing what the weights mean in general. Write the Wold representation without committing to a particular form for the weights, calling them \(\psi_i\):

\[ y_t = \psi_0 w_t + \psi_1 w_{t-1} + \psi_2 w_{t-2} + \cdots = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_i\, w_{t-i} \tag{4.10}\]

The model holds at every date, so Equation 4.10 can be shifted forward by \(j\) periods, replacing \(t\) with \(t+j\) throughout:

\[ y_{t+j} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_i\, w_{t+j-i} \tag{4.11}\]

Writing out the first terms makes the structure visible:

\[ y_{t+j} = \psi_0\, w_{t+j} + \psi_1\, w_{t+j-1} + \cdots + \psi_j\, w_{t} + \psi_{j+1}\, w_{t-1} + \cdots \tag{4.12}\]

The shocks run backward from date \(t+j\): the first term carries the shock of that same date, the second the shock of one period earlier, and so on. The generic term \(\psi_i\, w_{t+j-i}\) carries the shock of date \(t\) when

\[ t+j-i = t \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; i = j \tag{4.13}\]

There is exactly one such term, \(\psi_j\, w_t\). Every other term in Equation 4.12 carries a shock of a different date and is therefore unaffected by a change in \(w_t\), so differentiating leaves

\[ \frac{\partial\, y_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t} = \psi_j \tag{4.14}\]

This holds for any autoregressive order: the dynamic multiplier at horizon \(j\), the Wold weight \(\psi_j\), and the impulse response at horizon \(j\) are three names for the same number. For the AR(1), \(\psi_j = \phi^{\,j}\), and Equation 4.14 reduces to Equation 4.5.

The whole problem therefore collapses into a single question: what are the \(\psi_i\)?

4.4.2 The lag operator

The tool that answers it is the lag operator \(L\), which is an instruction rather than a number: it shifts a series back one period.

\[ L\,y_t = y_{t-1}, \qquad L^{2} y_t = y_{t-2}, \qquad L^{k} y_t = y_{t-k} \tag{4.15}\]

Its usefulness comes from the fact that, although it acts on series, it can be manipulated as if it were an ordinary algebraic symbol. A problem about recursions becomes a problem about polynomials.

Applied to the AR(1), substituting \(y_{t-1}=L y_t\) and collecting \(y_t\) on the left:

\[ y_t - \phi L\, y_t = w_t \;\Longrightarrow\; (1-\phi L)\, y_t = w_t \tag{4.16}\]

The bracket is the model itself, condensed into a single object. Call it \(\phi(L) = 1-\phi L\).

The Wold representation can be written the same way. Replacing each lagged shock by \(w_{t-i}=L^{i}w_t\) and collecting \(w_t\):

\[ y_t = \left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2} L^{2} + \phi^{3} L^{3} + \cdots\right) w_t \tag{4.17}\]

Call this second polynomial \(\psi(L)\). The two are now easy to relate. Substituting Equation 4.17 into Equation 4.16, the equality must hold whatever the sequence of shocks, so the two polynomials must multiply to one:

\[ (1-\phi L)\left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2} L^{2} + \cdots\right) = 1 \tag{4.18}\]

This is quick to check, and the argument is the same one used for the geometric series earlier. Expanding the two factors separately,

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 \cdot \left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2}L^{2} + \cdots\right) &= 1 + \phi L + \phi^{2}L^{2} + \phi^{3}L^{3} + \cdots\\[2pt] -\phi L \cdot \left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2}L^{2} + \cdots\right) &= \phantom{1 + {}} -\phi L - \phi^{2}L^{2} - \phi^{3}L^{3} - \cdots \end{aligned} \]

every term cancels against its counterpart in the line above, and only the leading \(1\) survives.

So \(\psi(L)\) is the inverse of \(\phi(L)\), in exactly the sense in which \(1/5\) is the inverse of \(5\): the polynomial that multiplied by \(\phi(L)\) gives one. The model can then be divided through:

\[ y_t = \frac{1}{\phi(L)}\, w_t = \psi(L)\, w_t \tag{4.19}\]

The two polynomials are the two directions of the same road. \(\phi(L)\) strips the dynamics out of the series and leaves the shock, subtracting from \(y_t\) precisely the part that was predictable from its own past. \(\psi(L)\) runs the other way, rebuilding the series from its shocks. The AR form describes \(y_t\) through its own past, the MA form through the shocks: same process, two readings, and the second is the one in which the impulse response is written in plain sight.

4.4.3 AR(p): the weights follow a recursion

The polynomial method is indifferent to the number of lags. For an AR(\(p\)),

\[ \phi(L) = 1-\phi_1 L - \phi_2 L^{2} - \cdots - \phi_p L^{p}, \qquad \phi(L)\, y_t = w_t \tag{4.20}\]

Here the inverse is not known in advance, so its coefficients are recovered one at a time by imposing \(\phi(L)\,\psi(L)=1\). The method is called matching coefficients, and rests on a simple fact: two polynomials are equal only if they agree on every power of \(L\). Since the right-hand side is the constant \(1\), it carries a coefficient of one on \(L^{0}\) and zero on every other power, so each of those must vanish in the product.

Taking the AR(2) as the smallest case, with \(\phi(L)=1-\phi_1 L-\phi_2 L^{2}\) and \(\psi(L)=\psi_0+\psi_1 L+\psi_2 L^{2}+\cdots\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L^{0}: \quad & \psi_0 = 1\\[3pt] L^{1}: \quad & \psi_1 - \phi_1\psi_0 = 0 &&\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_1 = \phi_1\\[3pt] L^{2}: \quad & \psi_2 - \phi_1\psi_1 - \phi_2\psi_0 = 0 &&\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_2 = \phi_1^{2}+\phi_2\\[3pt] L^{3}: \quad & \psi_3 - \phi_1\psi_2 - \phi_2\psi_1 = 0 &&\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_3 = \phi_1^{3}+2\phi_1\phi_2 \end{aligned} \tag{4.21}\]

Each line collects every way of producing that power of \(L\): for \(L^{2}\), the term \(1\cdot\psi_2\), the term \(-\phi_1 L\cdot\psi_1 L\), and the term \(-\phi_2 L^{2}\cdot\psi_0\). The pattern that emerges is general:

\[ \psi_0 = 1, \qquad \psi_i = \phi_1\psi_{i-1} + \phi_2\psi_{i-2} + \cdots + \phi_p\psi_{i-p} \tag{4.22}\]

with the convention \(\psi_j=0\) for \(j<0\). Every weight is a combination of the preceding ones, weighted by the coefficients of the model itself.

The result has a direct reading. Take \(\psi_2 = \phi_1^{2}+\phi_2\): a shock that arrived two periods ago reaches today by two routes, either passing twice through the first lag, which contributes \(\phi_1\times\phi_1\), or jumping straight through the second lag, which contributes \(\phi_2\). The total response is the sum of the available paths, exactly as in the bivariate system of the opening example, where a disturbance travelled through both the own lag and the cross lag at once.

The AR(1) is the degenerate case of Equation 4.22. With only \(\phi_1\) present the recursion reduces to \(\psi_i = \phi\,\psi_{i-1}\): every weight is the previous one times the same factor, which iterated from \(\psi_0=1\) gives back the pure powers \(\psi_i=\phi^{\,i}\). With two or more lags each weight mixes several of the preceding ones, and the closed form disappears.

One consequence is worth noting: distant lags do not affect early responses. In an AR(3) the weights \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are identical to those of an AR(2), because \(\phi_3\) only enters from \(\psi_3\) onward. A third lag cannot influence the response before three periods have passed.

4.4.4 Why more lags matter: the shape of the response

Since the impulse response is the sequence \(\psi_0,\psi_1,\psi_2,\dots\), changing the recursion changes the shape of the response itself. Two numerical examples make the point.

A hump. An AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.6\), \(\phi_2=0.3\) gives

\[ 1,\;\; 0.60,\;\; 0.66,\;\; 0.576,\;\; 0.544,\;\dots \]

The response at horizon 2 is higher than at horizon 1. An AR(1) with the same \(\phi_1=0.6\) would give \(1,\,0.60,\,0.36,\,0.216,\dots\), monotonically decaying, as \(\phi^{\,i}\) always must. A hump-shaped response, where the peak effect arrives with delay rather than on impact, is simply not available with a single lag. It is, however, exactly what macroeconomic data display: a monetary policy shock reaches its maximum effect on inflation several quarters later, not immediately.

A damped cycle. An AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.6\), \(\phi_2=-0.5\) gives

\[ 1,\;\; 0.60,\;\; -0.14,\;\; -0.384,\;\; -0.160,\;\; 0.096,\;\; 0.138,\;\dots \]

The response stays positive for a while, turns negative for a while, then positive again, fading as it goes: a genuine wave with a period of roughly six. This differs from the sign-flipping of an AR(1) with \(\phi<0\), where the sign alternates at every step. Damped cycles arise when the roots of \(\phi(z)=0\) are complex, and they are the mechanism through which a linear model generates business-cycle dynamics.

Wold weights possible IRF shapes
AR(1) \(\phi^{\,i}\), closed form monotone decay, or sign flip at every step
AR(2) two-term recursion also humps and damped cycles
AR(\(p\)) \(p\)-term recursion progressively richer shapes

This is why macroeconometric work rarely settles for a single lag: with \(p=1\) neither delayed transmission nor cyclical behaviour can be represented.

4.4.5 Stationarity, restated in a form that generalises

For the construction to be well defined the infinite sum \(\sum_i \psi_i w_{t-i}\) must converge, which requires the weights to die out. For the AR(1) the condition was \(|\phi|<1\), but there is an equivalent statement that carries over to any order.

Consider the polynomial \(\phi(z)=1-\phi z\), where \(z\) is now an ordinary complex number rather than the operator. It vanishes at \(z=1/\phi\), and requiring that root to lie outside the unit circle, \(|1/\phi|>1\), is precisely \(|\phi|<1\). The two conditions say the same thing about the AR(1).

The detour is worth taking because for an AR(\(p\)) there is no single coefficient to inspect, only a whole polynomial. The condition becomes: all roots of

\[ 1-\phi_1 z-\phi_2 z^{2}-\cdots-\phi_p z^{p} = 0 \tag{4.23}\]

lie outside the unit circle, or equivalently all inverse roots lie inside it, which is the form estimation software plots.[^stationarity]

This is also the form that survives the move to the multivariate case. In Part B the scalar polynomial \(\phi(L)\) becomes a matrix of polynomials \(G(L)\), the inverse \(\psi(L)\) becomes \(G(L)^{-1}=\sum_i \Psi_i L^{i}\), the recursion Equation 4.22 becomes a recursion in matrices, and the root condition Equation 4.23 becomes a condition on \(\det G(z)\). Nothing conceptual is added: scalars become matrices.

4.5 WIP Part B - The matrix case: VAR(1)

Everything above now repeats with one substitution: scalars become matrices, the logic is the same.

4.5.1 Recursive substitution for the VAR

The reduced-form VAR(1) (again dropping the constant) is the matrix twin of Equation 4.1, with \(Z_t\) stacking the \(n\) variables, \(\Phi\) an \(n\times n\) coefficient matrix, and \(w_t\) the vector of innovations:

\[ Z_t = \Phi\, Z_{t-1} + w_t \tag{4.24}\]

Substituting, exactly as before, every \(\phi^{\,i}\) becoming \(\Phi^{\,i}\):

\[ Z_t = \Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{t}\Phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} \tag{4.25}\]

Same structure as Equation 4.3: a transient \(\Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1}\) plus a weighted sum of past shock vectors, now with matrix weights \(\Phi^{\,i}\).

4.5.2 The VMA(\(\infty\)): the multivariate Wold representation

Letting the process run from the infinite past, if the VAR is stationary the transient dies (\(\Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1}\to 0\)) and

\[ Z_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Psi_i\, w_{t-i}, \qquad \Psi_i = \Phi^{\,i},\;\; \Psi_0 = I_n \tag{4.26}\]

This is the VMA(\(\infty\)), the multivariate Wold representation. For a VAR(1) the moving-average matrices are simply the powers \(\Psi_i=\Phi^{\,i}\) the literal analogue of the scalar \(\phi^{\,i}\). For a general VAR(\(p\)) the same object is obtained with the lag operator: writing the model as \(G(L)\,Z_t = w_t\) and inverting the matrix polynomial,

\[ Z_t = \mu + G(L)^{-1} w_t = \mu + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Psi_i\, w_{t-i}, \tag{4.27}\]

where the \(\Psi_i\) are the coefficient matrices of the power series \(G(L)^{-1}=\sum_i \Psi_i L^{i}\) (with \(\Psi_0=I_n\)), and \(\mu\) is the mean, restored once the constant is put back.

The parallel with the scalar case is one-to-one:

AR(1) — scalar VAR(p) — matrix
dynamic equation \(y_t=\phi\, y_{t-1}+w_t\) \(Z_t=\Phi\, Z_{t-1}+w_t\)
recursive substitution \(y_t=\phi^{\,t+1}y_{-1}+\sum_i \phi^{\,i} w_{t-i}\) \(Z_t=\Phi^{\,t+1}Z_{-1}+\sum_i \Phi^{\,i} w_{t-i}\)
MA weight \(\phi^{\,i}\) \(\Psi_i\;\;(=\Phi^{\,i}\text{ if }p=1)\)
representation MA(\(\infty\)) VMA(\(\infty\))
stationarity \(\lvert\phi\rvert<1\) roots of \(\det G(L)=0\) outside the unit circle
multiplier \(=\) IRF \(\partial y_{t+j}/\partial w_t=\phi^{\,j}\) \(\partial Z_{t+j}/\partial w_t'=\Psi_j\)

4.5.3 The multiplier becomes the impulse response

Differentiating Equation 4.27 with respect to the shock vector, exactly as in the scalar case:

\[ \frac{\partial\, Z_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t'} = \Psi_j \tag{4.28}\]

Each matrix \(\Psi_j\) is the system’s response, \(j\) periods ahead, to a one-unit impulse in the shocks: its \((k,\ell)\) entry is the reaction of variable \(k\) to a shock in variable \(\ell\). Where the scalar multiplier \(\phi^{\,j}\) traced a single decaying (or oscillating, or explosive) path, \(\Psi_j\) now carries both own- and cross-effects, so responses can travel between variables, oscillate, and feed back through the system before dying out — the feedback loop of the previous chapter, made quantitative.

4.5.4 Where this leads

These \(\Psi_j\) are the reduced-form impulse responses: they answer “what happens if the reduced-form innovation \(w_{\ell t}\) moves by one unit?” But the innovations are correlated across equations, so a shock to one variable rarely arrives in isolation — which makes the reduced-form responses hard to read as clean economic experiments. Recovering structural impulse responses, where the shocks are mutually uncorrelated and interpretable, requires identifying the VAR — orthogonalizing the innovations. That is the subject of the next chapter, together with the forecast error variance decomposition, which uses these very same \(\Psi_j\) to split forecast uncertainty across shocks.

4.5.5 From innovations to shocks: the bridge to identification

Up to this point \(w_t\) has been called a shock, loosely. It is time to be precise, because the distinction is what the next chapter is built on.

What Equation 4.24 contains are innovations: a statistical object, defined as the one-step-ahead forecast error — the part of \(Z_t\) that cannot be predicted from its own past,

\[ w_t = Z_t - E\!\left[Z_t \mid Z_{t-1}, Z_{t-2}, \dots\right] \tag{4.29}\]

that is, the genuinely new information arriving at date \(t\) — hence the name. Nothing in this definition carries economic content: it is a residual, whatever OLS leaves unexplained equation by equation.

A structural shock, by contrast, is an economically meaningful disturbance: a monetary policy shock, a technology shock, a demand shock.

The gap between the two is not a matter of vocabulary, it is a matter of correlation. Reduced-form innovations are correlated across equations:

\[ E\!\left[w_t\, w_t'\right] = \Sigma, \qquad \Sigma \text{ generally non-diagonal} \tag{4.30}\]

so \(w_{1t}\) is not “the shock to the first variable”: when it moves, the other innovations typically move with it. Asking “what happens if \(w_{1t}\) increases by one unit, holding the others fixed?” describes an event that is never observed in the data — which is why the \(\Psi_j\) of Equation 4.28 are reduced-form impulse responses, correct as a description of the joint dynamics, but not readable as economic experiments.

Recovering structural impulse responses means turning correlated innovations into mutually uncorrelated, interpretable shocks. This is the identification problem: writing \(w_t = B\, u_t\), where the structural shocks \(u_t\) are orthogonal, and finding enough restrictions to pin down \(B\). Two routes are available — a recursive ordering (Cholesky) or restrictions drawn from economic theory — and they are the subject of the next chapter, together with the forecast error variance decomposition, which uses these very same \(\Psi_j\) to split forecast uncertainty across shocks.
