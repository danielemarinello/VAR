4  From AR to VAR

4.1 From chapter 3 to chapter 4, the bridge:

What happens to output and prices when a demand shock hits? How far do inflation and employment move after a monetary tightening, and for how long? What does a sudden rise in oil prices do to the rest of the system? These are the questions macroeconometrics is built to answer.
The reasons for building a VAR at all, beyond data description and forecasting, are structural inference (“What is the causal effect of a shock on a variable?”) and policy analysis (“What would happen if the policy maker were to follow a different rule?”), and both require the ability to trace how variables respond to a shock.

Chapter 3 showed why the reduced form cannot answer them as it stands. It is estimable, but its residuals are correlated: moving one moves the others as well, so no ceteris paribus interpretation is available and the effect of a single shock can never be isolated. A demand shock cannot be spoken of on its own, and the same holds for others. Recovering the structural shocks from the reduced-form ones is the task of identification, taken up in Chapter 5

Before that, this chapter builds the machinery that makes it possible to read the effect of a shock on macroeconomic variables. The VAR model is rewritten so that every value of the system appears as a weighted sum of past shocks, the weights being exactly the trajectory a single shock traces over time. Once that object is in hand, the reason identification matters becomes concrete: the trajectory is only economically meaningful if the shock being traced is a structural one.

Example:

To see the mechanism at work, consider a bivariate system in the spirit of the previous chapter, with the output gap and inflation each depending on their own lag and on the other’s lag. Everything is in equilibrium, all variables sitting at their long-run values, until a single shock arrives at horizon zero and hits one equation.

Following what happens next makes the propagation visible. At impact only the variable that was hit moves. One period later the disturbance has reached the other equation through the cross-lag, and from there it travels back, since the second variable feeds the first in turn. What began as a one-off disturbance to one equation becomes a trajectory for the whole system, and the interactive below traces it horizon by horizon.

The four sliders control the coefficients of the two equations: the two own coefficients govern how long each variable holds on to its own past, the two cross coefficients how strongly each equation feeds the other.

Three things are worth drawing out of this picture.

The transmission takes a period. In the animation the shock reaches the second variable only one horizon after impact, never at the moment it arrives. This is not a graphical convenience: it is the reduced form at work, whose right-hand side carries lags alone. The picture therefore assumes that no contemporaneous transmission occurs in either direction, an assumption that is convenient but almost certainly false, as the previous chapter argued. Restoring the within-period links means restoring the matrix \(B\), and choosing which of its off-diagonal entries to allow and which to set to zero is exactly what Chapter 5 will take up.

The cross-coefficients are what makes the system a system. Setting both of them to zero splits the model into two univariate AR processes that happen to be plotted on the same axes. A shock to the output gap then stays in the output gap, inflation never moves, and each variable decays at its own rate with nothing passing between them. What the multivariate structure adds is precisely the return channel: the disturbance leaves the equation it hit, comes back one period later altered by the other variable, and the response that results is not something either equation could have produced on its own. Its shape, the delay before the peak, the length of the tail, is a property of the system rather than of any single equation.

The choice of where the shock hits is itself the problem. The two buttons produce different trajectories, which is unsurprising: an inflation shock is not an output shock. What is worth noticing is what the button actually does. It moves one innovation by one unit and holds the other at zero. In the data that event never occurs, because the innovations are correlated: when one moves, the other typically moves with it. The button performs an experiment that the reduced form does not license, which is why these trajectories are reduced-form impulse responses, faithful as a description of the joint dynamics but not readable as the effect of an economic shock. Making the button legitimate, so that pushing one shock really does leave the others untouched, is what identification delivers.

Pushing the coefficients up, especially both cross-terms together, can send the responses growing instead of fading. The chart rescales itself, so the divergence is easy to miss at a glance: what to watch is whether the two lines approach the zero line or drift away from it. A stationary system always returns to rest, and a response that never dies out signals that stationarity has been lost. The formal condition behind this is stated later in the chapter.

4.2 How to trace the dynamic effect of a shock?

Recursive substitution, the dynamic multiplier, and the Wold representation (infinite moving-average) are usually taught as separate topics. They are not: they are three views of a single question “how does a shock propagate through time?” The answer comes first in the scalar AR(1) world, where every step is intuitive and easy to understand, and is then replayed in the matrix VAR world, where the algebra is heavier but the logic is identical. The reward is a clean bridge: the moving-average weights, the dynamic multipliers, and the impulse responses are the same object.

3 key definitions:

  1. Recursive substitution: unwinding the model until \(y_t\) is written in terms of past shocks.

  2. Dynamic multiplier: the effect of one shock, horizon by horizon. This is the impulse response.

  3. Wold representation: under the stationarity assumption the influence of the initial value fades to zero and only the shock sum survives, this is the moving-average form, whose coefficients are the multipliers of point 2.

4.3 Part A - The scalar case: AR(1)

4.3.1 Recursive substitution

Starting with a first-order autoregressive process (to keep it simple the constant is dropped, it would only have shifted the mean), with \(w_t\) a white-noise shock:

\[ y_t = \phi\, y_{t-1} + w_t, \qquad w_t \sim \text{WN}(0,\sigma^2) \tag{4.1}\]

The idea is to rewrite \(y_t\) in another form, by substituting the process repeatedly into itself. The model holds at every date, so \(y_{t-1}\) can itself be written in terms of \(y_{t-2}\), and \(y_{t-2}\) in terms of \(y_{t-3}\), and so on:

\[ y_{t-1} = \phi\, y_{t-2} + w_{t-1}, \qquad y_{t-2} = \phi\, y_{t-3} + w_{t-2}, \qquad \dots \]

Each substitution moves one period further back: a new shock drops out and the power of \(\phi\) rises by one, step after step, until the chain reaches the initial value \(y_{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \phi\, y_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &= \phi\,\underbrace{(\phi\, y_{t-2} + w_{t-1})}_{y_{t-1}} + w_t = \phi^{2} y_{t-2} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &= \phi^{2}\underbrace{(\phi\, y_{t-3} + w_{t-2})}_{y_{t-2}} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t = \phi^{3} y_{t-3} + \phi^{2}w_{t-2} + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \\[2pt] &\;\;\vdots \\[2pt] &= \phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1} + \phi^{\,t}w_0 + \cdots + \phi\,w_{t-1} + w_t \end{aligned} \tag{4.2}\]

Collecting the terms gives the general solution:

\[ y_t = \phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{t} \phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = \phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1} + w_t + \phi\, w_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi^{\,t} w_0 \tag{4.3}\]

The value of \(y_t\) splits into two pieces: the initial condition \(\phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1}\), and a weighted sum of every shock since the start, with weight \(\phi^{\,i}\) on the shock \(i\) periods ago.

4.3.2 The dynamic multiplier and impulse response function

Since Equation 4.3 writes \(y_t\) as a linear function of the shocks, the effect of a single shock can be read off directly. This is the key result obtained by rewriting the process through recursive substitution.

Restarting the count at an arbitrary date \(t\), following the same logic applied above:

\[ y_{t+j} = \phi^{\,j+1} y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{j}\phi^{\,i}\, w_{t+j-i} \tag{4.4}\]

and differentiating with respect to the shock \(w_t\):

\[ \frac{\partial\, y_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t} = \phi^{\,j} \tag{4.5}\]

This defines the dynamic multiplier: the response of \(y\), \(j\) periods ahead, to a one-unit shock today. Two features matter:

  1. It depends only on the horizon \(j\), not on the date \(t\): the system reacts to a shock in the same way no matter when it lands.
  2. This is exactly an impulse-response function, tracing \(\phi^{\,j}\) for \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) draws the system’s response to an impulse.

The shape depends entirely on \(\phi\):

  • \(0<\phi<1\): monotone geometric decay toward zero;
  • \(-1<\phi<0\): decay with alternating sign (damped oscillation);
  • \(\phi>1\): explosive monotone growth;
  • \(\phi<-1\): explosive oscillation.

The dividing line is \(|\phi|<1\): inside it a shock’s effect eventually dies out and the system is stable; outside it the effect grows without bound, the process is explosive. The borderline \(\phi=1\) is the random walk, where \(\phi^{\,j}=1\) for every \(j\) and a shock’s effect is permanent.

Move the slider through all four regimes — this is the response \(\partial y_{t+j}/\partial w_t = \phi^{\,j}\):

4.3.3 From recursive substitution to the Wold representation

Now let the process have been running not from date \(-1\) but from the infinite past, so, let \(t\to\infty\) in Equation 4.3. When \(|\phi|<1\) the term \(\phi^{\,t+1} y_{-1}\to 0\) vanishes, and only the shock sum survives:

\[ y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = w_t + \phi\, w_{t-1} + \phi^{2} w_{t-2} + \cdots \tag{4.6}\]

This is the MA(\(\infty\)), or Wold representation. Where the current value \(y_t\) is written as a weighted sum of all past shocks. It is nothing more than recursive substitution pushed to its limit under stationarity, and its weights \(\phi^{\,i}\) are exactly the dynamic multipliers of Equation 4.5.

The same object is reached algebraically with the lag operator \(L\) (with \(L\,w_t = w_{t-1}\)): writing Equation 4.1 as \((1-\phi L)\,y_t = w_t\) and inverting,1

\[ y_t = \frac{w_t}{1-\phi L} = \Big(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\phi^{\,i} L^{i}\Big) w_t, \tag{4.7}\]

which reproduces Equation 4.6 term by term. The geometric series converges for exactly the reason that \(|\phi|<1\).

4.3.4 (WIP!) For curious mind: from AR(1) to AR(2), AR(3),…, AR(n)

So far the Wold representation has been obtained by recursive substitution, and its weights turned out to be the powers \(\phi^{\,i}\). This result is specific to the AR(1). This section introduces the tool that handles any number of lags (the lag operator) and shows what changes, and what does not, when moving to an AR(\(p\)).

4.3.4.1 The weights are the impulse response, whatever the model

Write the Wold representation with generic weights \(\psi_i\):

\[ y_t = \psi_0 w_t + \psi_1 w_{t-1} + \psi_2 w_{t-2} + \cdots = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_i\, w_{t-i} \tag{4.8}\]

The model holds at every date, so Equation 4.8 can be shifted forward by \(j\) periods simply by replacing \(t\) with \(t+j\) everywhere:

\[ y_{t+j} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_i\, w_{t+j-i} \tag{4.9}\]

Writing out the first terms makes the structure visible:

\[ y_{t+j} = \psi_0\, w_{t+j} + \psi_1\, w_{t+j-1} + \psi_2\, w_{t+j-2} + \cdots + \psi_j\, w_{t} + \psi_{j+1}\, w_{t-1} + \cdots \tag{4.10}\]

The shocks run backward from date \(t+j\): the first term carries the shock of that same date, the second the shock of one period earlier, and so on. The date of the shock in the generic term is \(t+j-i\), so the term containing the shock of date \(t\) is the one for which

\[ t+j-i = t \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; i = j \tag{4.11}\]

There is exactly one such term, and it equals \(\psi_j\, w_t\). Every other term in Equation 4.10 carries a shock of a different date, and is therefore unaffected by a change in \(w_t\). Differentiating,

\[ \frac{\partial\, y_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t} = \psi_j \tag{4.12}\]

This holds for any autoregressive order: the dynamic multiplier at horizon \(j\), the Wold weight \(\psi_j\), and the impulse response at horizon \(j\) are three names for the same number. For the AR(1), \(\psi_j = \phi^{\,j}\), and Equation 4.12 reduces to Equation 4.5.

The whole problem therefore collapses into a single question: what are the \(\psi_i\)?

4.3.4.2 The lag operator

The lag operator \(L\) is an instruction, not a number: it shifts a series back one period.

\[ L\,y_t = y_{t-1}, \qquad L^{2} y_t = y_{t-2}, \qquad L^{k} y_t = y_{t-k} \tag{4.13}\]

Applied to the AR(1), substituting \(y_{t-1}=L y_t\) and collecting \(y_t\):

\[ y_t - \phi L\, y_t = w_t \;\Longrightarrow\; (1-\phi L)\, y_t = w_t \tag{4.14}\]

The bracket is the model itself, rewritten with everything on the left. Call it \(\phi(L) = 1-\phi L\).

The Wold representation can be written the same way. Replacing each lagged shock by \(w_{t-i}=L^{i}w_t\) and collecting \(w_t\):

\[ y_t = \left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2} L^{2} + \phi^{3} L^{3} + \cdots\right) w_t \tag{4.15}\]

Call this second polynomial \(\psi(L)\). Substituting Equation 4.15 into Equation 4.14, the equality must hold for any sequence of shocks, so the two polynomials multiply to one:

\[ (1-\phi L)\left(1 + \phi L + \phi^{2} L^{2} + \cdots\right) = 1 \tag{4.16}\]

The product is immediate to verify: multiplying by \(1\) reproduces the series, multiplying by \(-\phi L\) reproduces it shifted up one degree and with opposite sign, and the two cancel term by term except for the leading \(1\).

So \(\psi(L)\) is the inverse of \(\phi(L)\), in exactly the sense in which \(1/5\) is the inverse of \(5\): the polynomial that multiplied by \(\phi(L)\) gives one. The model can then be “divided through”:

\[ y_t = \frac{1}{\phi(L)}\, w_t = \psi(L)\, w_t \tag{4.17}\]

The two polynomials are the two directions of the same road. \(\phi(L)\) strips the dynamics out of the series and leaves the shock — it subtracts from \(y_t\) precisely the part that was predictable from the past. \(\psi(L)\) runs the other way, rebuilding the series from its shocks. The AR form describes \(y_t\) through its own past; the MA form describes it through the shocks. Same process, two readings — and the second is the one in which the impulse response is written in plain sight.

4.3.4.3 AR(p): the weights follow a recursion

Recursive substitution still works with more lags, but it becomes unmanageable: with two lags there are two \(y\) terms to chase at every step, with three lags three. The polynomial method, on the other hand, is indifferent to the order.

For an AR(\(p\)),

\[ \phi(L) = 1-\phi_1 L - \phi_2 L^{2} - \cdots - \phi_p L^{p}, \qquad \phi(L)\, y_t = w_t \tag{4.18}\]

The inverse \(\psi(L)=\psi_0+\psi_1 L+\psi_2 L^{2}+\cdots\) is no longer known in advance, so its coefficients are recovered one at a time by imposing \(\phi(L)\psi(L)=1\). Since the constant polynomial \(1\) has zero coefficient on every power of \(L\), matching coefficients degree by degree gives

\[ \psi_0 = 1, \qquad \psi_i = \phi_1\psi_{i-1} + \phi_2\psi_{i-2} + \cdots + \phi_p\psi_{i-p} \tag{4.19}\]

with the convention \(\psi_j=0\) for \(j<0\). For an AR(2), explicitly:

\[ \psi_1 = \phi_1, \qquad \psi_2 = \phi_1^{2}+\phi_2, \qquad \psi_3 = \phi_1^{3}+2\phi_1\phi_2 \tag{4.20}\]

The interpretation is transparent: \(\psi_2\) collects the two routes by which a shock two periods old reaches today — through the first lag twice (\(\phi_1^2\)), or through the second lag directly (\(\phi_2\)).

The AR(1) is the degenerate case of Equation 4.19. With only \(\phi_1\) present the recursion becomes \(\psi_i = \phi\,\psi_{i-1}\): every weight is the previous one times the same factor, which iterated from \(\psi_0=1\) gives the pure powers \(\psi_i=\phi^{\,i}\). With two or more lags each weight mixes the previous ones, and the closed form disappears.

One consequence worth noting: distant lags do not affect early responses. In an AR(3), \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are identical to those of an AR(2), because \(\phi_3\) only enters from \(\psi_3\) onward — a third lag cannot influence the response before three periods have passed.

4.3.4.4 Why more lags matter: the shape of the response

Since the impulse response is the sequence \(\psi_0,\psi_1,\psi_2,\dots\), changing the recursion changes the shape of the response itself. Two numerical examples make the point.

A hump. An AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.6\), \(\phi_2=0.3\) gives

\[ 1,\;\; 0.60,\;\; 0.66,\;\; 0.576,\;\; 0.544,\;\dots \]

The response at horizon 2 is higher than at horizon 1. An AR(1) with the same \(\phi_1=0.6\) would give \(1,\,0.60,\,0.36,\,0.216,\dots\) — monotonically decaying, as \(\phi^{\,i}\) always must. A hump-shaped response, where the peak effect arrives with delay rather than on impact, is simply not available with a single lag. It is, however, exactly what macroeconomic data display: a monetary policy shock reaches its maximum effect on inflation several quarters later, not immediately.

A damped cycle. An AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.6\), \(\phi_2=-0.5\) gives

\[ 1,\;\; 0.60,\;\; -0.14,\;\; -0.384,\;\; -0.160,\;\; 0.096,\;\; 0.138,\;\dots \]

The response stays positive for a while, turns negative for a while, then positive again, fading as it goes — a genuine wave with a period of roughly six. This is different from the sign-flipping of an AR(1) with \(\phi<0\), where the sign alternates at every step. Damped cycles arise when the roots of \(\phi(z)=0\) are complex, and they are the mechanism through which a linear model generates business-cycle dynamics.

Wold weights possible IRF shapes
AR(1) \(\phi^{\,i}\), closed form monotone decay, or sign flip at every step
AR(2) two-term recursion also humps and damped cycles
AR(\(p\)) \(p\)-term recursion progressively richer shapes

This is why macroeconometric work rarely settles for a single lag: with \(p=1\) neither delayed transmission nor cyclical behaviour can be represented.

4.3.4.5 Stationarity, restated in a form that generalises

For the construction to be well defined, the infinite sum \(\sum_i \psi_i w_{t-i}\) must converge: the weights have to die out. For the AR(1) the condition is \(|\phi|<1\), but there is an equivalent statement that carries over to any order. The polynomial \(\phi(z)=1-\phi z\) — with \(z\) a complex number, not the operator — vanishes at \(z=1/\phi\), and requiring that root to lie outside the unit circle, \(|1/\phi|>1\), is precisely \(|\phi|<1\).

The detour is worth taking because for an AR(\(p\)) there is no single coefficient to inspect, only a whole polynomial. The condition becomes: all roots of

\[ 1-\phi_1 z-\phi_2 z^{2}-\cdots-\phi_p z^{p} = 0 \tag{4.21}\]

lie outside the unit circle — equivalently, all inverse roots lie inside it, which is what estimation software plots.2

This is also the form that survives the move to the multivariate case. In Part B the scalar polynomial \(\phi(L)\) becomes a matrix of polynomials \(G(L)\), the inverse \(\psi(L)\) becomes \(G(L)^{-1}=\sum_i \Psi_i L^{i}\), the recursion Equation 4.19 becomes a recursion in matrices, and the root condition Equation 4.21 becomes a condition on \(\det G(z)\). Nothing conceptual is added: scalars become matrices.

4.4 Part B - The matrix case: VAR(p)

Everything above now repeats with one substitution: scalars become matrices, the logic is the same.

4.4.1 Recursive substitution for the VAR

The reduced-form VAR(1) (again dropping the constant) is the matrix twin of Equation 4.1, with \(Z_t\) stacking the \(n\) variables, \(\Phi\) an \(n\times n\) coefficient matrix, and \(w_t\) the vector of innovations:

\[ Z_t = \Phi\, Z_{t-1} + w_t \tag{4.22}\]

Substituting, exactly as before, every \(\phi^{\,i}\) becoming \(\Phi^{\,i}\):

\[ Z_t = \Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{t}\Phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} \tag{4.23}\]

Same structure as Equation 4.3: a transient \(\Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1}\) plus a weighted sum of past shock vectors, now with matrix weights \(\Phi^{\,i}\).

4.4.2 The VMA(\(\infty\)): the multivariate Wold representation

Letting the process run from the infinite past, if the VAR is stationary the transient dies (\(\Phi^{\,t+1} Z_{-1}\to 0\)) and

\[ Z_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\,i}\, w_{t-i} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Psi_i\, w_{t-i}, \qquad \Psi_i = \Phi^{\,i},\;\; \Psi_0 = I_n \tag{4.24}\]

This is the VMA(\(\infty\)), the multivariate Wold representation. For a VAR(1) the moving-average matrices are simply the powers \(\Psi_i=\Phi^{\,i}\) the literal analogue of the scalar \(\phi^{\,i}\). For a general VAR(\(p\)) the same object is obtained with the lag operator: writing the model as \(G(L)\,Z_t = w_t\) and inverting the matrix polynomial,

\[ Z_t = \mu + G(L)^{-1} w_t = \mu + \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Psi_i\, w_{t-i}, \tag{4.25}\]

where the \(\Psi_i\) are the coefficient matrices of the power series \(G(L)^{-1}=\sum_i \Psi_i L^{i}\) (with \(\Psi_0=I_n\)), and \(\mu\) is the mean, restored once the constant is put back.

The parallel with the scalar case is one-to-one:

AR(1) — scalar VAR(p) — matrix
dynamic equation \(y_t=\phi\, y_{t-1}+w_t\) \(Z_t=\Phi\, Z_{t-1}+w_t\)
recursive substitution \(y_t=\phi^{\,t+1}y_{-1}+\sum_i \phi^{\,i} w_{t-i}\) \(Z_t=\Phi^{\,t+1}Z_{-1}+\sum_i \Phi^{\,i} w_{t-i}\)
MA weight \(\phi^{\,i}\) \(\Psi_i\;\;(=\Phi^{\,i}\text{ if }p=1)\)
representation MA(\(\infty\)) VMA(\(\infty\))
stationarity \(\lvert\phi\rvert<1\) roots of \(\det G(L)=0\) outside the unit circle
multiplier \(=\) IRF \(\partial y_{t+j}/\partial w_t=\phi^{\,j}\) \(\partial Z_{t+j}/\partial w_t'=\Psi_j\)

4.4.3 The multiplier becomes the impulse response

Differentiating Equation 4.25 with respect to the shock vector, exactly as in the scalar case:

\[ \frac{\partial\, Z_{t+j}}{\partial\, w_t'} = \Psi_j \tag{4.26}\]

Each matrix \(\Psi_j\) is the system’s response, \(j\) periods ahead, to a one-unit impulse in the shocks: its \((k,\ell)\) entry is the reaction of variable \(k\) to a shock in variable \(\ell\). Where the scalar multiplier \(\phi^{\,j}\) traced a single decaying (or oscillating, or explosive) path, \(\Psi_j\) now carries both own- and cross-effects, so responses can travel between variables, oscillate, and feed back through the system before dying out — the feedback loop of the previous chapter, made quantitative.

For a bivariate VAR(1) this is a \(2\times2\) grid: each variable’s reaction to each shock. The sliders are the four entries of \(\Phi\); each panel traces an entry of \(\Psi_j=\Phi^{\,j}\) over the horizon \(j\):

viewof phi11 = Inputs.range([-0.95, 0.95], {value: 0.5, step: 0.05, label: "Phi 1,1 (x on own lag)"})
viewof phi12 = Inputs.range([-0.95, 0.95], {value: 0.3, step: 0.05, label: "Phi 1,2 (x on lagged y)"})
viewof phi21 = Inputs.range([-0.95, 0.95], {value: 0.2, step: 0.05, label: "Phi 2,1 (y on lagged x)"})
viewof phi22 = Inputs.range([-0.95, 0.95], {value: 0.5, step: 0.05, label: "Phi 2,2 (y on own lag)"})
{

  const Phi = [[phi11, phi12], [phi21, phi22]];
  const mul = (A, B) => [
    [A[0][0]*B[0][0] + A[0][1]*B[1][0], A[0][0]*B[0][1] + A[0][1]*B[1][1]],

Try pushing the coefficients up: some combinations put a root of \(\det G(L)\) inside the unit circle and the responses explode instead of dying out — nonstationarity, made visible.

4.4.4 Where this leads

These \(\Psi_j\) are the reduced-form impulse responses: they answer “what happens if the reduced-form innovation \(w_{\ell t}\) moves by one unit?” But the innovations are correlated across equations, so a shock to one variable rarely arrives in isolation — which makes the reduced-form responses hard to read as clean economic experiments. Recovering structural impulse responses, where the shocks are mutually uncorrelated and interpretable, requires identifying the VAR — orthogonalizing the innovations. That is the subject of the next chapter, together with the forecast error variance decomposition, which uses these very same \(\Psi_j\) to split forecast uncertainty across shocks.

4.4.5 From innovations to shocks: the bridge to identification

Up to this point \(w_t\) has been called a shock, loosely. It is time to be precise, because the distinction is what the next chapter is built on.

What Equation 4.22 contains are innovations: a statistical object, defined as the one-step-ahead forecast error — the part of \(Z_t\) that cannot be predicted from its own past,

\[ w_t = Z_t - E\!\left[Z_t \mid Z_{t-1}, Z_{t-2}, \dots\right] \tag{4.27}\]

that is, the genuinely new information arriving at date \(t\) — hence the name. Nothing in this definition carries economic content: it is a residual, whatever OLS leaves unexplained equation by equation.

A structural shock, by contrast, is an economically meaningful disturbance: a monetary policy shock, a technology shock, a demand shock.

The gap between the two is not a matter of vocabulary, it is a matter of correlation. Reduced-form innovations are correlated across equations:

\[ E\!\left[w_t\, w_t'\right] = \Sigma, \qquad \Sigma \text{ generally non-diagonal} \tag{4.28}\]

so \(w_{1t}\) is not “the shock to the first variable”: when it moves, the other innovations typically move with it. Asking “what happens if \(w_{1t}\) increases by one unit, holding the others fixed?” describes an event that is never observed in the data — which is why the \(\Psi_j\) of Equation 4.26 are reduced-form impulse responses, correct as a description of the joint dynamics, but not readable as economic experiments.

Recovering structural impulse responses means turning correlated innovations into mutually uncorrelated, interpretable shocks. This is the identification problem: writing \(w_t = B\, u_t\), where the structural shocks \(u_t\) are orthogonal, and finding enough restrictions to pin down \(B\). Two routes are available — a recursive ordering (Cholesky) or restrictions drawn from economic theory — and they are the subject of the next chapter, together with the forecast error variance decomposition, which uses these very same \(\Psi_j\) to split forecast uncertainty across shocks.
